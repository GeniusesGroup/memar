# Test
This document states Memar's position on **Test**: a Test is the written expectations of a named Process, written before the work and kept as a standing part of the system that owes them — not a debugging aid, not a runner's output, and not a claim about code quality. Its concept-level definition is owned by [Process → Expectations and Checks](../../process.md#expectations-and-checks); this document adds what the protocol layer owns. It states that the expectation precedes the enactment, and why: the ordering is what lets a check reach the errors of understanding rather than only the errors of implementation. It states that the form the expectation takes is fixed by the domain's checks, so a building's requirement and a module's protocol surface are governed by the same rules in different artifacts. It states that a Test is a deliverable a system owes, what it may assume, and what a passing suite does and does not establish. And it records the software ecosystem's name for this discipline, **Test-Driven Development**, only to set the name down: that tradition's account of a Test differs from this one, and the difference is substantive rather than terminological. It deliberately states no structural rule — where a Test lives and what its artifacts are called are Rules, carried in the implementing module's `rules/` folder per [Rule → Where Rules Live](../../rule.md#where-rules-live-rules-beside-protocol), and pointed to from [What a Test Is Not](#what-a-test-is-not) rather than restated here.

## Introduction

### Motivation
Three failures recur wherever a system under this protocol develops, and each is a failure of the Test's standing rather than of its content.

The first is **the Test as a debugging session's residue**. Expectations are written after the work when the work misbehaves, kept until the defect is fixed, and then discarded with the branch. Nothing remains, so the next change to the same behavior starts from no stated expectations at all, and the defect that prompted them is free to return. [Process → Expectations and Checks](../../process.md#expectations-and-checks) names the writing-after-the-fact activity verification rather than the discipline; what this document adds is the consequence at the level of a system. An expectation that does not outlive the session that produced it never became part of the system.

The second is **the word doing three jobs at once**. A domain's word for a Test commonly names the written artifact, the act of running it, and the verdict it produces. Every document that uses one word for all three forces its reader to re-derive which is meant, and an agent that guesses wrong will report a green suite as a claim about quality it never made. The separation is stated in this document because a protocol has to be checkable against something, and a protocol that cannot name what it governs cannot be conformed to.

The third is **the Test with no named subject**. A file that exercises code without stating which Process's expectations it holds cannot be reviewed, cannot be shown to be redundant, and cannot be shown to be missing. The commonest reason a system's expectations turn out to be uncovered is that nobody can say what the full set of expectations is. Binding every Test to a named Process is what makes the coverage question answerable at all.

A fourth failure is specific to a system that has adopted the discipline but stated no Rule: **three incompatible layouts and no runner**. Such a system may hold a test artifact beside each thing it tests in one area, a dedicated folder in another, and neither in a third, with no stated criterion for which applies. Where its own language has no runner, every test artifact present may be inert. That is a Rule's subject, not this document's, and it is named here because a protocol whose structural form is unstated leaves exactly this failure open.

### Methodology
The document consumes [Process](../../process.md) for the definition of an expectation, a check, and their ordering, which is the concept layer's and is not restated here. It adds to that concept what the protocol layer owns: the obligation a system carries, the standing an artifact keeps, the form the expectation takes when the domain's checks are not executable, and the boundary between what a check concludes and what a Test is. The reading of an umbrella requirement as the developed Process's expectations is stated here rather than consumed, because the requirement concept it reads has no document of its own to own it. Where the sources for these claims come from is recorded in the paired researches, each cited at the claim it supports.

Its own contribution is drawn from the obligations a deliverable carries in this project's existing protocols. [SDK → Part of the module, not beside it](../modules/sdk.md#part-of-the-module-not-beside-it) states that a service's client side is part of the module's protocol surface rather than an accessory beside it, and this document applies that shape to the Test. [Rule → Who Owns and Introduces Rules](../../rule.md#who-owns-and-introduces-rules) and [Rule → Where Rules Live](../../rule.md#where-rules-live-rules-beside-protocol) fix where the structural conditions belong, which is why this document carries none. [Linter → A rule's home is its subject's document](../computer/linter.md#a-rules-home-is-its-subjects-document) fixes the authorship of those conditions.

Structural conditions are excluded from this document as a standing rule, not as an omission. A statement of where a Test lives changes what an organization does with its source tree, is checkable against a tree, and is replaceable by an organization that organizes its tests differently. That is [Rule → What a Rule Is](../../rule.md#what-a-rule-is) in every particular, and stating it here would both duplicate the definition and put a replaceable condition in a document that owns no condition it can relax.

## Explanation

### What a Test Is
A **Test** is **the written form of a named Process's stated expectations, kept as a part of the system that owes them.** Three commitments are inseparably in that definition, and each is inherited rather than invented here.

The first is that a Test is a **written expectation**. [Process → Expectations and Checks](../../process.md#expectations-and-checks) defines an expectation as belonging to the process model, preceding every enactment, and defines a check as the comparison of an instance against it. This document uses *Test* for the artifact holding those expectations and takes *check* for the comparison, so the two words never name the same thing.

The second is that a Test is **of a Process**. A Test is not of a function, a file, a class, or a service; those are participants in a Process or the surface through which it is enacted. Naming the Process is what makes the Test reviewable against the document that defines the Process, and it is what lets a reader answer the coverage question, per [A Test's Subject Is a Named Process](#a-tests-subject-is-a-named-process).

The third is that a Test is **kept**. That word carries the standing this document exists to state: a Test is not consumed by the run that exercised it and does not expire with the branch that introduced it. What it is kept for is stated in [A Test Is Part of the System That Owes It](#a-test-is-part-of-the-system-that-owes-it).

### Test, Check, and Runner
Three things carry the word in practice, and the protocol is written against the first. Keeping them apart is what lets a system conform to the protocol without conforming to any particular tool.

1. **The Test**, the artifact: the written expectations, in whatever form the domain's checks can be applied. This is what the protocol governs.
2. **The check**, the comparison of a Process instance against a Test. [Process → Expectations and Checks](../../process.md#expectations-and-checks) owns the concept; a check exists wherever an instance can be compared, whether or not anything mechanical performs the comparison.
3. **The runner**, the mechanism that finds Tests, executes the checks, and reports outcomes. A mechanism, chosen per [Process → Process Before Mechanism](../../process.md#process-before-mechanism) after the expectations exist. The protocol binds none of this: no framework, no discovery scheme, no file layout, no output format.

The failure this ordering prevents is the substitution of one for another. A suite that is green establishes that the checks passed. Treating that as evidence about the second, or about the third, is the confusion this section exists to stop, and [What a Passing Suite Establishes](#what-a-passing-suite-establishes) states the substitution's limits directly.

### A Test's Subject Is a Named Process
Every Test names the Process whose expectations it holds, and that name is the one the document defining the Process uses. A Test whose subject cannot be named is not an incomplete Test; it is not a Test, because there is nothing for it to be an expectation of.

The binding runs downward only. A Test derives its authority from the document that defines its Process, and a Test that contradicts that document is asserting a different Process than the one the system claims to realize. [TDD → Relationship to Other Concepts](./test.md#relationship-to-other-concepts) names that failure as testing the wrong process. Where a Test appears to require the Process to behave otherwise, the resolution is a change to the Process's definition, recorded as a change, and never an edit to the Test alone.

The binding also answers the coverage question. A system's stated obligations are enumerable, being each part of its protocol surface whose expectations are decidable before enactment per [Process → Expectations and Checks](../../process.md#expectations-and-checks), and a Test is present or absent against that enumeration. This is what makes the question of coverage a question with an answer rather than a matter of taste, and it is the whole content of the obligation stated next.

### A Test Is Part of the System That Owes It
A system that owes expectations owes a Test for them. The Test is part of the system that has the obligation, delivered with it, on the same footing as the rest of that system's protocol surface. That is the delivery consequence [SDK → Part of the module, not beside it](../modules/sdk.md#part-of-the-module-not-beside-it) states for a service's client side, applied here. A system whose protocol surface is complete but whose expectations live outside it, in a developer's own practice, has an unrealized surface: the vacancy is filled by whichever participant happens to remember, and the system's conformance claim rests on memory.

Three boundaries keep this obligation from becoming a target, because an obligation stated as a number stops being an obligation and becomes a quota.

- **The obligation is over Processes, not lines.** What is owed is a Test for each part of the protocol surface whose expectations are decidable, not a number of Test files, not a percentage of statements, and not a line-coverage figure. A system with one Test covering a whole surface and a system with fifty Tests covering fragments of it may both be complete; a system with a high coverage figure and no Test for its error contract is not.
- **A non-decidable expectation is not exempt, it is differently formed.** A process whose enactment is a one-shot, per [Process → Expectations and Checks](../../process.md#expectations-and-checks), still states its expectations, and the Test holds them in whatever form that domain applies: a stated acceptance condition, a defined observation procedure, a written record of what must hold. The form varies; the standing does not.
- **The obligation is a floor, not a ceiling.** A system that has a Test for every decidable expectation has met this protocol and may stop. Nothing here requires further tests, and [What a Passing Suite Establishes](#what-a-passing-suite-establishes) states why adding them past that point buys nothing the protocol can name.

### What a Test May Assume
A Test states expectations **of the contract its subject's defining document states, and of nothing else.** It may assume what the Process's definition promises and may not assume how an implementation happens to satisfy it. Three consequences follow, and each names a failure the boundary prevents.

- **A Test does not assert an implementation's incidental structure.** What a method is called internally, how many allocations a path makes, what order an implementation happens to observe, and what a private helper returns are not the contract unless the defining document says they are. A Test that asserts one of these binds the system to an accident, and the next conforming implementation then fails a check that was never about the contract.
- **A Test that must reach past the contract has found a gap, not a workaround.** If satisfying a checkable expectation requires reaching something the contract does not promise, the expectation belongs to a contract the system has not stated. The correct response is to state the contract, not to widen the Test's reach.
- **A Test holds no state that decides the next Test's outcome.** A Test's verdict follows from the instance under check and the expectations it states, not from what an earlier Test left behind. Ordering dependence between Tests makes the suite's verdict a property of the order rather than of the system, which removes the verdict's meaning as evidence.

### The Expectation Is Written Before the Work
The written form of a named Process's expectations is produced **before** the enactment that is judged against it, and that ordering is not a preference about workflow. Its ground is reach.
The written form of a named Process's expectations is produced **before** the enactment that is judged against it, and that ordering is not a preference about workflow. Its ground is reach.

**Before the expectation, the thing whose expectation it is.** A Test states the expectations of a named Process, and the Process's own contract is stated before that: the model, then the protocol or abstraction that declares what the contract is, then the Test that states the contract's expected input and output in checkable form, then the enactment. The order is not a formality of stages but a dependency of authority. A Test written against code that has no stated contract is not early — it is late, because it has already decided what the contract is by writing the code, and the document it should have been written from does not exist yet. [Modeling → Modeling Before Implementation](../../modeling.md#modeling-before-implementation) states the general principle that commitments become explicit before they become costly; the same principle runs through the layers here, and a Test inherits its authority from the document above it rather than supplying it.

That order is why the abstraction is not a later refinement of a tested behavior. The abstraction declares the contract; the Test states the contract's expectations; the implementation satisfies both. A Test that disagrees with the abstraction tests a different Process, and an implementation that satisfies the Test while violating the abstraction has satisfied the wrong thing — which is why [A Test's Subject Is a Named Process](#a-tests-subject-is-a-named-process) binds a Test downward to its defining document rather than to the code it exercises.

A check that draws only on the implementation can find an error of implementation. It cannot tell a wrong implementation from a wrong specification, because the two look identical from inside the implementation and the check has nothing else to compare against. [Test Research 002 → The ordering](./test.research.002.md) records the taxonomy Goodenough and Gerhart built for exactly this: construction errors, specification errors, design errors, and requirements errors are four distinguishable kinds, and a test set can detect any of them only in so far as its selection criterion reflects the specifications written at the earlier stages. Without those, the check is confined to the implementation and the errors of understanding — the expensive ones, the ones that are still there after the code is fixed — become invisible.

For a development in particular the ordering has no escape, because the base topic's condition holds without exception: conformance to a target stage always matters, since a target stage no participant can determine has been reached is not a target stage. There is therefore no development for which stating the expectation first is optional. For processes whose conformance does not matter, it is optional, and a process that cannot be re-enacted is not thereby exempt: it states its expectations before it runs, and the fact that the check afterward may be unavailable limits the check's availability, not the ordering.

That is the whole reason the expectation leaves the author's head before the work begins. An expectation held only in the mind of the actor is one person's private model, and a private model is neither reviewable by another participant nor decidable by any check. Writing it down does not make it provable and does not make it true; it makes it *shared*, and sharing is what a check operates on. The ordering therefore binds wherever a check is expected to reach a named Process at all, and it is not weakened by the fact that no check establishes that a system is free of defects — see [What a Green Suite Establishes](#what-a-passing-suite-establishes), which is a separate question.

Three boundaries keep this from becoming a formality, and each is the failure it is stated against.

- **The expectation is of the developed Process, not of the developers' procedure.** What must be stated is the input the Process will receive, the output it must produce, the conditions each applies under, and the outcomes that count as which — of the thing being built, not of the way the building is being done. Stating how the team will work while leaving the developed Process's input and output unstated satisfies this document in appearance and inverts it in substance. The one written expectation serves both jobs: checking the instance against it also decides whether the development step is done.
- **An expectation revised to match what was built is not an expectation any more.** It describes the instance rather than the intent, and the next check has no subject. A revision made after the enactment, recorded as a change, and made *before* the next enactment is a different act from an edit that quietly absorbs whatever appeared.
- **The developer is not the Process's audience.** A Test must satisfy what [Test Practice → The Procedure](./test.practice.md) calls the another-agent step: another participant, reading it, could run the check without asking its author what was meant. An expectation private to its author has not left tacit holding.

### A Test Is Documentation, and Comment Is the Last Place to Look
A Test states what a thing must do and can be checked against doing it. A comment states what a thing does and can be checked against nothing. That difference in what can be checked is the start of the preference, but not the whole of it, because the comparison is not Test against comment — it is Test against every other place the same sentence could have gone, and this repository has already decided where each of those is.

**Where each kind of statement belongs.** Four destinations, and the order is the order of how well each can hold what it is given.

- **What must hold is a Test.** Nothing else can be checked against it, and an expectation that is not checked is a claim.
- **What a thing is, and what a reader needs to read about it, belongs to the thing.** [Khayyam → Self-Documenting Code and Naming](../../khayyam/khayyam.md#self-documenting-code-and-naming) states the mechanism for a language that has one: human-facing text is a value a method writes into an influenced variable, so the type that owns the behavior owns that text and another language is another method on that type rather than a prose line above the declaration — the same family [Type → Human-facing identity](../../type.md#human-facing-identity) places on the Type. What the grammar can carry, it carries, so that opaque code is structurally impossible rather than merely discouraged: primitives are wrapped in named capsules, generic containers are replaced by domain-specific ones, and a scope that a reader would otherwise have to be told about is **named**. `vr i uint8` under a comment explaining what `i` means is the case this dissolves — write `sc` for the scope and its name is its explanation, and the sentence never exists to drift.
- **Why a decision was made, which alternative was rejected, and on what ground belongs to the artifact's own structured companion**, decided by the facet that owns the kind of statement: a change and its reasons in the [Changelog](../../documentation-changelog.md), a bounded inquiry and what it found in a [Research](./test.research.001.md) record, live unresolved state in a [Handoff](../../documentation-handoff.md). Each is a file that has a lifecycle, participants, and a rule about what belongs in it. A comment is not a smaller version of any of them and has none of those properties — it cannot record when the decision was made, who argued it, or what was considered and not done, and nothing will ever compare it against the code.
- **A comment is what remains when none of the above can hold**, and in this repository it is a fallback the direction of travel is away from: [Comment Policy → Keep it or drop it](../../../modules/khayyam/rules/comment-policy/comment-policy.md) records that an organization may forbid comments outright and require a type's human-facing text to come from its own methods, and that what may not happen by accident is the middle state where comments become a permanent fallback while the methods are never written.

**The failure that decides the order.** A statement that has drifted from the thing it describes still reads fluently and is believed by exactly the reader who most needs it to be accurate. Nothing fails, nothing compares the sentence to the code, and the drift surfaces later without evidence that it was ever right. A Test that has drifted **fails**, which is the outcome the system was built to produce when a stated expectation is not met; a companion document that has drifted is at least a document whose owner and date are recorded. So wherever a fact has a destination that can be checked, kept current, or attributed, a comment is the worse home for it, and putting it there buys nothing that the destination did not already give.

**The boundary on this claim.** "An uncommented file is itself documentation" holds where the language's declarations carry the meaning — where a reader learns what a thing is from its name, its type, and its scope rather than from a sentence about it — and that is a property of the language's specificity, not a moral about comments. Where it does not hold, an uncommented file is merely terse. The measured evidence runs the same way: Basili and Selby disclose that the code reading in their experiment ran on uncommented programs and call it "a worst-case scenario for code reading," which is a statement that prose does affect what a reader finds. So the claim is not that comments are useless. It is that **a fact with a destination that can check it does not belong in a comment**, and that where a language can carry a fact in its own declarations, the comment is a fallback the language was designed to make unnecessary.
### The Form the Expectation Takes Is the Domain's
This protocol is not limited to computer code, and the limitation is not a courtesy extension. A Test's subject is a Process, and Processes are not software.

A building's employer's requirement is a Process's expectations, and it is written the way such requirements are written — as a Test, before the building is raised. What a construction project demonstrates is that **the form of a Test is fixed by what the domain's checks can be applied to, and that form may be a model rather than a sentence.** A drawing or a building-information model is how a textual requirement becomes something two parties can compare: the requirement is expressed as geometry, geometry is what gets checked, and the disagreement that would otherwise sit in the reading of a sentence moves into the comparison of a thing both parties can see. The model is not documentation of the expectation; it *is* the expectation, in the one form this domain's checks can be applied to.

The same reasoning covers every domain whose checks are not mechanical. A checkout counter's ergonomics, an organization's process, a document's acceptance condition: each states its expectations in whatever form makes them decidable — a defined procedure with defined observations, a stated acceptance condition, a geometric model, a written record of what must hold. What binds in all of them is the ordering and the checkable form. What varies is the artifact's shape, and no shape is more a Test than another.

Where Memar's own documentation is the developed artifact, the discipline applies to itself: this document's claims are checkable expectations for how Memar's development is conducted, and they are held to the same standard as any other subject's.

### An Umbrella Requirement Is a Developed Process's Expectations
Requirements concepts at feature scale are not a separate artifact standing beside a Test, and they are not merely input to one. A user story, under this protocol's reading, **is** the written input-and-output expectations of the Process being developed toward: who or what brings what in, what must come out, under which conditions, with which outcomes. Where those expectations can be executed by a machine, the executable Test is their most precise realization, and writing it is the ordinary path rather than an extra.

The consequence is that a narrated desire is not a requirement. "It should feel fast" names no input, no output, and no condition, so nothing can be checked against it and no coverage question has an answer. A requirement that stops at narration fails this protocol however well it is phrased; one that states checkable expectations for the Process it names satisfies it. The same reading applies to any equivalent requirements concept in any domain Memar is applied to — a drawing schedule, a bill of quantities, a contract's acceptance schedule — because the question is always the same one: can another participant run the check without asking what was meant?

### The Ecosystem's Name for This Is TDD, and This Protocol Does Not Adopt It
What this protocol does **not** adopt from that tradition is the ecosystem's *reduction* of the discipline to its most frequent mechanism — as if the discipline were the red-green-refactor cycle, a test framework, or a file layout. Those are realization choices, appropriate where they fit and for software development entirely ordinary, and none of them is constitutive of anything stated here. What binds is the ordering and the checkable form; the tools are selected afterward, per project and per check, and are not this document's subject.

The software ecosystem calls the discipline **Test-Driven Development**, and the name is worth recording only to be set down. Two reasons, and the second is the substantive one.

The first is narrow. "TDD" names a practice the way a protocol name does, and this protocol binds Processes that no such practice reaches. Binding it here would make a building's requirement sound like an eccentric application of a software term, which is the impression the previous paragraph exists to remove.

The second is that the ecosystem's own account of the term differs from this protocol's, and the difference is not cosmetic. [Test Research 002 → TDD names a different thing](./test.research.002.md) records Beck's formulation: "If you don't drive development with tests, what do you drive it with? Speculation? Specifications." For that account a Test is the driver *in place of* a specification. For this protocol a Test **is** the specification, and the older literature it draws on — Howden, Goodenough and Gerhart — made the same identification, with the consequence that a Test remains owed to the system afterwards rather than being spent on the step that produced it. Beck also records that TDD "isn't a testing technique" at all but "an analysis technique, a design technique, really a technique for all the activities of development," which is his own acknowledgement that the word names the wrong activity.

A system may be developed under either discipline and will be developed well if it meets either. What this protocol declines is the dependency: it states what a Test is and what is owed, in its own terms, and does not require that those terms be reconciled with a name whose established meaning is a different one.

### What a Passing Suite Establishes
A green run establishes exactly one thing: **every check the run performed, on the instance it performed them against, matched the expectations that check states.** Read strictly, that sentence rules out the three substitutions that make a green run carry more than it can.

It does **not** establish that the system conforms to its protocol. Conformance is a property of an implementation against the rules of the protocol it claims to realize, and it is broader than any suite, per [Protocol → Protocols and External Observers](../../protocol.md#protocols-and-external-observers). A suite can be green while a part of the surface has no Test at all, and under [A Test Is Part of the System That Owes It](#a-test-is-part-of-the-system-that-owes-it) that is the failure this protocol most cares about. The two questions are asked separately, being whether each stated expectation holds and whether every decidable expectation is stated, and a run answers only the first.

It does **not** establish that the expectations are the right ones. A Test can state an expectation faithfully and state the wrong one. [TDD → A User Story Is a Developed Process's Expectations](./test.md#an-umbrella-requirement-is-a-developed-processs-expectations) is about the umbrella requirements concept becoming concrete, not about a machine confirming the concrete form was the right one. A green suite narrows the space of what has not been examined; it does not examine it.

It does **not** establish anything about behavior the checks did not exercise. This is the boundary a percentage figure most often blurs: a coverage number is a statement about which parts were reached, never about what was verified where they were reached, and never about what would happen on a path nothing reached. The number is not evidence; the named expectation is.

### What Would Take This Further, and What Would Not
The literature states a result that reads like a contradiction of the section above, and it is stated here because a reader who has met it will otherwise suspect this document of having missed it.

The result: **a test can be a proof.** Goodenough and Gerhart proved that properly structured tests can demonstrate the absence of errors, given that the test set's data-selection criterion is both reliable and valid. Howden's algebraic construction reaches a related conclusion: a test set defined against a class of hypothesized correct programs witnesses membership in that class, and under a valid class "it is possible to prove correctness by testing."

It does not contradict the section above, and the reason is the condition. What licenses the conclusion is a proved property **of the selection**, not the careful writing of expectations — a completeness argument about the test set itself, which is a stronger object than a Test as this protocol defines it. A Test here states a named Process's expectations and is required to say nothing about whether its set is complete. So the result is not reachable by writing tests well, no Test this protocol asks for claims to reach it, and no suite this protocol obliges a system to keep becomes a proof by carrying more expectations.

Two further results bear on this document and are recorded with it rather than left for a reader to find.

Internal structure is a second determinant of what a check can reach, alongside the specification. Dijkstra's argument places it there: what can be established about a program "is not purely a function of the program's external specifications and behaviour but depends critically upon its internal structure." A system satisfying this protocol in full may therefore still be wrongly built. This document says nothing about structure and does not claim to; the boundary is stated so that a complete Test suite is not read as a complete account of a system's correctness. Note what it is *not*: it is a statement about what a check concludes, not about whether expectations must be written down, and EWD 249 sets aside the question of design methodology by name.

And the limit on a Test's verdict being evidence that its expectations were right has independent support from a direction this protocol did not anticipate. What carries the information is not the verdict but the Test's existence as a second reading of the specification: testers "look at the specifications from an entirely different point of view than do the developers" and so find "flaws in the logic of the design or incompleteness in the specification" (Duke 1975), and Hoare, reviewing his own earlier opposition to testing, concluded that "the success of tests is that they test the programmer, not the program" (2003). Both are consistent with [What a Test Is Not](#what-a-test-is-not): a Test's value is that it makes the expectations sayable a second time, which is a property of the artifact and not of its result.

### Test, Verification, and Inquiry
Three activities involve comparing what exists against what was wanted, and the protocol binds one of them. Naming them apart is routing, and the discipline's own boundaries are stated by the documents that own them.

- **Development under the discipline** is the case the protocol governs: a Test states a Process's expectations and the instance is checked against them, in the ordering the ecosystem's TDD, recorded under its own topic requires.
- **Verification** is the case where the instance already exists and expectations are stated afterward to check it. [Process → Expectations and Checks](../../process.md#expectations-and-checks) classifies it as a different activity: valuable, and without the ordering that lets a check catch a departure from stated intent. A verification's artifact is still a Test and still carries the same obligations; what it does not carry is the discipline.
- **Inquiry** is the case where the enactment is the means of discovery and there is nothing yet to state expectations about. Expectations belong to the inquiry process itself, being what evidence it must produce, what decision it must return, and what record it must leave, and the subject is not a Test. [Process → Defect Resolution as an Inquiry](../../process.md#defect-resolution-as-an-inquiry) governs that process; the fix an inquiry concludes with is a development step under this protocol.

### What a Test Is Not
Four things are not a Test, and each is mistaken for one often enough to be worth naming.

- **Not a runner's report.** The output of a run is an outcome about the checks that ran. A report is a mechanism's output; the Test is the artifact the mechanism read, and the artifact exists whether or not anything ran it.
- **Not a coverage figure.** A figure is a statement about which parts were reached. It cannot distinguish a Process whose every decidable expectation is stated from one whose expectations are numerous and wrong, so it cannot stand for either.
- **Not a structural rule.** Where a Test lives, what its artifacts are named, and how a runner finds them are [Rules](../../rule.md#what-a-rule-is): stated conditions an owner introduces, checkable against a tree, and replaceable by an organization that organizes its tests differently. They are carried in the implementing module's `rules/` folder beside its `protocol/` folder, and this document neither states them nor restates them. The one pointer this document owes them is here, and it is a single sentence: **a system that has adopted this protocol has not thereby adopted a layout**, and a reader looking for one is looking for a Rule, not for a gap in this document.
- **Not a claim about the developers' conduct.** A suite's state is evidence about a system. Whether the expectations were written before the work is established by the discipline the ecosystem's TDD, recorded under its own topic requires, not by a passing run, and a green suite produced after the fact is verification however thoroughly it passes.

### Relationship to Other Concepts
**The ecosystem's Test-Driven Development.** [The Ecosystem's Name for This Is TDD](#the-ecosystems-name-for-this-is-tdd-and-this-protocol-does-not-adopt-it) states what this document takes from that tradition and what it declines. Its content is not restated here, and the divergence it records is the reason this document is independent of the name rather than a specialization of it.

**Process.** The concept layer, consumed and not restated. This document's entire content is downstream of [Process → Expectations and Checks](../../process.md#expectations-and-checks) and [Process → Development as a Process](../../process.md#development-as-a-process): a Test exists because a Process has expectations that precede its instances, and a system owes Tests because development produces instances of Processes whose expectations were decidable in advance. What the protocol layer adds is the obligation and the form; what Process supplies is the reason a check has a subject at all.

**Protocol.** This document is itself an instance of the pattern it states: a named set of declarative rules governing a process — here, development — declared before enactment. Its governed process is not confined to one place in a system's life: the same rules apply wherever that process runs. The authorship rule of [Linter → A rule's home is its subject's document](../computer/linter.md#a-rules-home-is-its-subjects-document) applies to what this document governs: rules about how Test expectations are written and checked live here, while the structural conditions about where a Test lives are Rules in a module's `rules/` folder, and rules about what the developed system must do live in the documents that own those subjects.

**Modeling.** [Modeling → Modeling Before Implementation](../../modeling.md#modeling-before-implementation) and this protocol are two applications of one principle — commitments become explicit before they become costly. The division of labor is unchanged: the model defines what the Process is and what its expectations must be checked against; the written expectation states those expectations in checkable form, with its authority derived from the model. A Test that contradicts the model tests the wrong Process.

**Rule.** The owner of every structural condition about Tests. [Rule → Where Rules Live](../../rule.md#where-rules-live-rules-beside-protocol) places an owner's executable conditions in a Module's `rules/` folder beside its `protocol/` folder, and this document is the subject those Rules cite rather than the place they are stated. That separation is what lets an organization keep a layout this repository would not choose and still conform to this protocol.

**Modularity.** [Modularity → Capability Completeness](../../modularity.md#capability-completeness) requires that the Rules governing a relationship belong with the Module that owns that relationship; the parallel requirement here is that the Tests of a Module's protocol surface belong with that Module. A Test kept outside its system is the same vacancy in the same place.

**Linter.** An enforcement surface, not an author. Per [Linter → A rule's home is its subject's document](../computer/linter.md#a-rules-home-is-its-subjects-document), the linter owns the check and the subject's document owns the claim; the Rules this document points to are the claims, and whether an organization turns them on is per its linter configuration.

**SDK.** The closest structural precedent in this repository for the obligation stated in [A Test Is Part of the System That Owes It](#a-test-is-part-of-the-system-that-owes-it): a deliverable whose absence is a half-realized surface, filled in by whoever gets there first. What differs is direction. An SDK is delivered outward to consumers; a Test is delivered inward as the system's own evidence about itself.
