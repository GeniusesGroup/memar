## Question and Goals
### Questions
1. What does this repository actually do with `sc` today — in the grammar, in the compiler, in the rules, in the protocols, and in the `.kh` sources? — **Answered**
2. What do other languages give a branch body / deferred block, does any give it a concept distinct from a function value, and what is the *type* of such a thing called in each type system? — **Answered**
3. If a branch body is "just a function value wearing a hat", what in Khayyam can play the role of the function type, given that the language has no primitive types, no literal, and no construction form? — **Answered**
4. What are the real options, what does each cost, and which does each piece of evidence favour and how strongly? — **Answered**
5. Do we actually need the concept of a code scope, or could `mt` alone carry the need? — **Answered** (the owner ruled on 2026-10-02 that the code scope stays, on grounds of what the language can express rather than on what removal would cost; see [Addendum](#addendum--the-ruling-the-closure-finding-and-what-the-cost-argument-was-not))
6. Does the question as asked decompose into a grammar question and a base-layer category question that the evidence bears on differently? — **Answered**
7. **(A)** If there is no `sc` at all, **what mechanism performs flow management** — not what it would cost, but what actually takes one branch instead of the other — given that the grammar ships no control-flow keyword, no operator and no literal? — **Answered**, and the answer changed twice. The second pass inferred that no mechanism in the grammar can produce a jump. The third pass read the toolchain's own emitted code and found the mechanism is an ordinary **call**: the branch body is emitted as a function, handed to the driver as a value, and the driver calls it. It also found that **the deciding half is not written yet** — the driver is a body-less method with no link entry.
8. **(B)** Why do other languages have a code scope implicitly, in `if` itself? Could they have said it is unnecessary and that a plain function does the job as well? Which languages pass a conditional body as a value and which execute it in place, and did any language manage to say "a conditional body is just a function value" while keeping a conditional? — **Answered**, and the second pass's answer here was **wrong and is withdrawn**. Its claim that "a language that passes a conditional body as a value has no conditional statement to pass it to" confuses a language's having a conditional with a conditional's *body* being a value. Every language examined has both a conditional and function values; what none does is let the `if` body *be* one. The decisive case the second pass missed is **Tcl**, whose `if` is a *command* whose body is an argument — that is Khayyam's design, and it works.
9. **(C)** Is a block's access to the enclosing variables exotic or ordinary — and if it is ordinary, why is a document silent where every other language states the rule, and does `docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md`'s prohibition actually conflict with what the compiler does? — **Answered.** The owner is right that reach is ordinary; the finding is the silence. The prohibition and the capability do not conflict, and the question of which to keep is settled: both stand unchanged and a third sentence — the statement of the reach — is what is owed.
10. What does the researcher recommend, on what ground, and concretely what does flow management look like if `sc` goes? — **Answered**, on a ground that is neither of the two earlier ones. The first pass made the capability's exoticness decisive; the second made "a branch must be performed by the grammar" decisive; **both are withdrawn**. The third rests on a single clause — Khayyam has ruled that flow constructs are library methods — and therefore a library method's branch-body argument must be a *named, typed, declared* thing. The direction is unchanged. The owner had not accepted the previous version's recommendation and has not accepted this one.

## Findings
Every claim in this section is one of three kinds, and the kind is marked: **Evidence** (a document or a fetched source says it, at the line or URL given), **Measurement** (this research's own scan or read of the code), **Inference** (this research's reasoning from evidence, which the owner may reject).

### The sources supply no evidence about `sc`, either way
**Measurement.** Over the 1197 `.kh` files under `modules/`:

- Methods whose influencing group names a scope — **0** in any `.kh` file. The only declaration anywhere in the tree that puts a branch body in a signature is a TypeScript test fixture: `modules/khayyam/targets/js/test/emit.test.ts:15` declares `tp Scope cp {`, and `:23`, `:29`, `:33`, `:35`, `:39` write `(condition Condition, branch Scope)`.
- Types whose name denotes a scope — **0** in any `.kh` file.
- Methods whose influencing group names a method — **1**, and it is refused: `modules/khayyam/targets/js/bootstrap/behaviour.kh:15` writes `tp Emit mt (self Text) (body Method) ()`, and the recorded refusal is `unbound-type-name` (modules/khayyam/targets/js/target.handoff.md:106; modules/khayyam/targets/js/language-gaps.md:27 — "the language admits a method in an argument position … and no unit can declare the type that holds one").
- Commented witnesses of a scope-in-signature — **2 files, 4 lines**: `modules/computer/adt/protocol/listeners.kh:3` and `:6` (`// OnAbsent(call scope) (err Error)`, `// OnPresent(call scope) (err Error)`), and `modules/process/operation/protocol/listeners.kh:8` and `:12` (`// OnSuccess(call scope) (err Error)`, `// OnFailure(call scope) (err Error)`).
- Every other `\bsc\b` hit inside a `.kh` file is **not Khayyam**: 53 of them are Go receivers named `sc` in `modules/lib/net/http/headers/set-cookie.kh` (e.g. `:50`, `:85`, `:118`), which is archived Go source carrying a `.kh` extension and which the repository already treats as an archive rather than a yardstick (modules/khayyam/execution.research.001.md:70-71).

**Evidence.** The same state is recorded independently by three documents, all reached the same way: modules/process/control-flow/answers.md:66 ("No `.kh` file under `modules/` declares a method whose parameter is a scope; the only two `sc` declarations in the tree are the probe bodies at … `repeat.kh:25` and `compose.kh:20`"), modules/process/control-flow/answers.handoff.md:21, and modules/khayyam/targets/js/sc-in-signature.md:84.

**Inference.** The sources supply no evidence either way. It does not depend on `sc` in any way a removal would break: the only real declarations are probes whose entire purpose is to measure the language, so removing `sc` would delete the probe rather than any program. That is a fact about a body of source that has never been written in the language's final syntax, and it is not evidence that `sc` is unnecessary — a body of source that used nothing would say the same about every construct.

### What the compiler already implements
**Measurement.** `sc` is not one option among several in the frontend; it is load-bearing in the command set, the keyword set, the semantic representation, and the backend's lowering.
- The keyword set: `modules/khayyam/core/src/scan.ts:6` — `export const SUBTYPE_KEYWORDS = ["in", "cp", "mt", "ab", "sc"] as const;`
- The semantic representation: `modules/khayyam/core/src/sr.ts:1` — `export type Subtype = "in" | "cp" | "mt" | "ab" | "sc";` — and `sr.ts:82`, `form: "tp-sc"`, and `sr.ts:117`, the refusal label `scope-placement`.
- The body reader: `modules/khayyam/core/src/body.ts:13-23` declares `Command` as exactly four forms — `declare`, `scope`, `invoke`, `return` — and the doc comment at `body.ts:6-12` says the set is "the whole of what a body may contain" because the grammar ships no control-flow keyword, operator, or literal. `body.ts:15` is the `scope` command. `body.ts:176-195` is `readScope`, whose own comment at `:176` reads "`sc` is the one subtype a body admits", and whose `:190` refuses any other subtype. `body.ts:200` dispatches `tp` in a body straight to `readScope`.
- The file-level refusal: `modules/khayyam/core/src/parse.ts:342-343` refuses a top-level `sc` with the label `scope-placement`.
- The lowering: `modules/khayyam/targets/js/src/emit.ts:423-426` emits a scope as `function $Name$() { … }` declared inside the method that holds it; `emit.ts:46-49` and `:70-76` lift a block's cells and its scopes out of command order so a driver may name a scope declared below it; `emit.ts:223-226` refuses a scope used as a call's receiver; `emit.ts:344` and `:356` give a scope the local name `$${name}$`; `targets/js/src/program.ts:230-231` handles the `tp-sc` form; `targets/js/src/link.ts:42` records the convention that "a type no program names as a value — a scope's own" is the function the compiler emitted for it.
- The tests that pin it: `bundles/khayyam/core/tests/body.test.ts:83-98` ("a scope is a command holding the commands in it") and `:120-131` (the ordering test, whose third command drives a scope); `core/tests/form.test.ts` carries the in-body accept row and `core/tests/refusal.test.ts` the top-level refuse row named at modules/khayyam/rules/scope-placement/scope-placement.md:23.
- The reader and its display: `modules/khayyam/lsp/src/blocks.ts:21` (`kind: "cp" | "ab" | "mt" | "sc"`), `lsp/src/body-declaration.ts:56-62` (a body declaration is a scope only if `subtype.text === "sc"`), `lsp/src/binding-table.ts:21` (`"tp-sc": { role: "scope", origin: "a Scope declared in this file" }`), `lsp/src/diagnostics.ts:25` (the `scope-placement` message), and the display contract's `scope` role with `implementedBy: "grammar-scope"` and scope `entity.name.scope.khayyam` in `modules/khayyam/display/display.json` (role entry at `:241-252`) with the grammar rule that emits it at `.agents/vscode/extensions/khayyam-language/syntaxes/khayyam.tmLanguage.json:142-147`.

**Inference — the sharpest compiler consequence.** Removing `sc` removes the *only* declaration form a body admits. `body.ts:200` shows why this is not a small edit: inside a body, `tp` has no other meaning, so with `readScope` gone the reader must refuse `tp` in a body outright, and a body would be left holding exactly three commands — `vr`, an invocation, and `return`. A Khayyam body would then have no way to *declare a named thing* at all except a variable. That is not a smaller language; it is a language in which the only named body is a method's own, which is the condition the grammar was written to avoid.

### The four places a removal would strike, and one more nobody has named
**Evidence.** `sc` is load-bearing at four documented levels, and the first is the load-bearing one.

1. **The Type category.** `docs/type.md:181-188` states Scope as one of four categories: "A Scope is a Type that delimits a semantic boundary" (`:182`), naming five realizations including "a local algorithmic block (as used by `if`, `loop`, `goto`)" (`:184`); `:186` places placement with the realization, not the category; and `:188` makes the category conditional: "If Scope were merely a compiler-level syntactic convenience with no semantic significance, it would not qualify as a Type." `docs/type.md:191` puts the four categories on one level — "equally direct realizations of Type; none is nested inside another as a subtype" — and `:193-198` gives the model's only cross-category relation table, in which Scope has exactly one row: "commonly used within | Scope → Method | current Khayyam usage pattern, not a containment requirement".
2. **The grammar.** `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:126-131`: the form at `:127`; the definition at `:128`; the inertness at `:129` ("A code scope is inert until a library-provided method drives it: the grammar ships no control-flow keywords or logical operators of its own"); the lowering and `return` at `:130` ("There is no `goto` keyword: a compiler of this language lowers `sc`-driven branches to internal jumps, and `return` is an IR marker that must itself end with a line break so it cannot be written as `return 0`"); the type-level argument at `:131`. The subtype slot at `:70`, the keyword table row at `:43`, and Type Principles Realized at `:208`.
3. **The protocol.** `docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:32` ("Across realizations, the common denominator is the branch-grouping primitive"), `:34` ("A code scope names *what* would run; an explicit control-flow operation decides *when*"), `:37` ("may expose a generic conditional abstraction that receives an explicit branch scope"), `:134` ("the `sc` subtype and the compiler's jump/branch intrinsics"), and six `tp … sc` blocks in the three worked examples at `:39-57`, `:66-88`, `:90-118`. The same statement reaches the Linter (docs/protocols/computer/linter.changelog.md:17 — "the compiler emits `sc` entry/exit and jump events; DAA/linter should treat `sc` as the common denominator, not `IF` names") and the Compiler protocol (docs/protocols/computer/compiler.md:35 — "the grammar's control-flow primitive is the inert code scope `sc`", and `:38` — analysis tools subscribe to "entering and leaving a scope").
4. **The self-documenting claim.** `docs/protocols/process/test.md:103` cites `sc` by name as one of the mechanisms that make opaque code structurally impossible: "a scope that a reader would otherwise have to be told about is **named** … write `sc` for the scope and its name is its explanation, and the sentence never exists to drift."

**Evidence — a fifth nobody has named.** `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:145`, under *How Khayyam realizes Memory*, lists `sc` among the sources of lifetime correctness: "Correctness of lifetimes is obtained from sovereign encapsulation (no leaking raw internal references), **deterministic `sc` boundaries**, the absence of syntax-level pointers, definite-assignment analysis (an uninitialized `vr` is refused), and linter path coverage for release". No document in this repository's current state discusses what the Memory section's claim becomes if `sc` goes. **Inference:** this is the removal's most consequential unexamined cost, because it is a *safety* claim rather than a convenience one — with `sc` gone the section has four sources of lifetime correctness instead of five, and the deterministic-boundary term is the only one of the five that is a grammar-level construct.

### What this repository already believed, recorded before the question was asked

That is option (c)/(d) of this research, already considered and declined, with a stated reason. **Inference:** the reason it records is a *reader* reason, not a type reason — the distinction it protects is one a reader makes when looking at a declaration, and it is a property of the language as designed rather than of any file in this repository.
- `docs/protocols/process/control-flow.changelog.md:136` — "**No mainstream language provides `sc`-style scope abstractions as a first-class type.**" The prior-art claim the same changelog relies on is verified below, and it is **partly false**, which is itself a finding.
- `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:174` states the base-layer stake: "The grammar does express the modeling concepts defined at the base layer — `tp` for Type, `sc` for Scope — because those definitions are general and language-independent, and the grammar supplies only the declaration mechanism, not a specific contract." **Inference:** this sentence makes `sc` the *grammar's realization* of the base category, not the category itself; it does not say the category has no other realization.
- `docs/khayyam/khayyam.handoff.md:57` records an owner position adjacent to the question: "a scope's name is what a control-flow driver names before the scope exists — `CF.IF(condition, ifProcess, external_vr)` on the line above the `tp ifProcess sc` that declares `ifProcess` — and control flow is a library, so the language's grammar will never carry that name in a place." **Inference:** the forward-reference requirement is what the two-pass reading in `emit.ts:46-49` exists to serve, and it is a requirement of *any* named-branch-body scheme; it is not an argument for or against `sc` as a category.

### What other languages give a deferred block
Each entry states the mechanism, whether it is a distinct concept or a function value, what the type is called, and what the call site costs. Quotations are verbatim from the fetched source; the URL is given per language. **Evidence** marks a claim a fetched source states; **Inference** marks this research's reading of it.

**Go — no distinct concept; a function value, and the function type is a first-class type.** `defer` does not take a block: "The expression must be a function or method call; it cannot be parenthesized" (https://go.dev/ref/spec, Language version go1.27). Function literals are the closure mechanism — "Function literals are closures: they may refer to variables declared in a surrounding function" — and their type is ordinary: "A function type denotes the set of all functions with the same parameter and result types" (same source). Labels are not types and not values: "In contrast to other identifiers, labels are not block scoped and do not conflict with identifiers that are not labels" (same source). Call-site cost of a deferred body: none beyond the call. **Inference:** Go spent no concept on the deferral and paid for it by forbidding a block there; a Khayyam `sc` is the thing Go cannot write.

**Rust — no distinct concept for the block, but a distinct type-level bound hierarchy for callability, and this is the most instructive language for Khayyam.** "A closure expression produces a closure value with a unique, anonymous type that cannot be written out" (https://doc.rust-lang.org/reference/types/closure.html). The hierarchy: "Closure types all implement `FnOnce` … Additionally, some closures implement more specific call traits" (same source), and https://doc.rust-lang.org/std/ops/trait.Fn.html: "Since both `FnMut` and `FnOnce` are supertraits of `Fn`, any instance of `Fn` can be used as a parameter where a `FnMut` or `FnOnce` is expected." Where nothing is captured, even the bound disappears: "Non-async, non-capturing closures can be coerced to function pointers (e.g., `fn()`) with the matching signature", with `type Binop = fn(i32, i32) -> i32;` (https://doc.rust-lang.org/reference/types/closure.html). **Inference — the decisive reading for Khayyam:** Rust needs `Fn`/`FnMut`/`FnOnce` because a closure's captures are *not written down* — the environment is implicit, so the type system has to constrain what may happen to it. A Khayyam `sc` writes its environment down: it is a named block in the enclosing method, so the variable it may reach is a name a reader sees (`docs/khayyam/khayyam.handoff.md:57`). **Inference:** what Rust's `Fn` bounds exist to constrain is precisely what Khayyam's `sc` makes explicit, which is an argument that the *bound* is unnecessary here, not that the *block* is.

**Kotlin — no distinct concept; a function type with its own notation, and a named class may implement it.** "Kotlin uses function types, such as `(Int) -> String`" and "`(A, B) -> C` denotes a type that represents functions that take two arguments of types `A` and `B` and return a value of type `C`" (https://kotlinlang.org/docs/lambdas.html). The class-implements-function-type form, which is the JVM's single-abstract-method mechanism: "Use instances of a custom class that implements a function type as an interface: `class IntTransformer: (Int) -> Int { override operator fun invoke(x: Int): Int = TODO() }`" (same source). Call-site cost, at its cheapest: "If the lambda is the only argument in that call, the parentheses can be omitted entirely: `run { println("...") }`", and the trailing-lambda convention, "if the last parameter of a function is a function, then a lambda expression passed as the corresponding argument can be placed outside the parentheses" (same source). **Inference:** Kotlin answers the owner's type question directly — a function type is *written in the type position with its own notation*, and where an organization wants a named thing behind it, a named class implements that type. Both halves of option (b) and option (d) are here, and neither needed a new category.

**Scala — not verified.** The Scala documentation could not be fetched from this machine. **Inference, flagged as such:** Scala shares Kotlin's JVM function-type notation and the class-implements-function-type form, and this repository's own prior-art record already places OCaml beside Scala at docs/type.md:191 ("OCaml's structure/signature distinction is similar but also separates functions from types"). Nothing about Scala is established here beyond that inference.

**Swift — both, and the second is the interesting one.** The function type is written in the type position — `func someFunction(callback: () throws -> Void) rethrows` (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/swiftlang/swift-book/main/TSPL.docc/ReferenceManual/Declarations.md). Whether a closure outlives the call is a property of the parameter, not a type: "A nested function is nonescaping if it captures a value that's guaranteed to never escape — such as an in-out parameter — or passed as a nonescaping function argument. Otherwise, the nested function is an escaping function" (same source). And the effect is in the *type*, so a caller can see whether the body may fail: "A function's type includes whether it can throw an error and what type of error it throws" (same source). **Evidence — and the closest mainstream analogue to `sc`:** Swift's deferred block is not a value at all. The same document writes `defer { x = localX }` (same source) as a statement whose body is a code block with no type, no parameter, and nowhere to pass it. **Inference:** Swift reaches for a *separate statement* for the deferral case precisely because a function value cannot express "run this when the enclosing scope ends" — a function value has no lexical attachment to the scope it was written in. That is `sc`'s entire content, and it is the one mainstream language examined that pays for a separate construct for it. The cost is the opposite of what the ecosystem usually pays: no call-site syntax for the block, and one more keyword in the statement set.

**Java — partially established only.** The Java Language Specification and the `java.util` API pages returned HTTP 403 from this machine; no primary Java source was fetched. What is established: anonymous functions exist in Java "Supported since Java 8" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anonymous_function, an encyclopedia source, used here only for that fact), and the JVM-family mechanism Kotlin's primary source documents — a named class implementing a function type (https://kotlinlang.org/docs/lambdas.html, quoted above). **Inference, flagged as unverified:** Java therefore needs a *named interface type* in the position, and the separate `Runnable` / `Callable` / `Supplier` interfaces exist because that interface's single method's signature is what the position admits — with the checked-exception tension that a `Callable` may throw and a `Runnable` may not. Nothing in this paragraph is evidence; it is the shape the Kotlin quotation implies, and it is carried as an open question below rather than as a finding.

**Lisp-family (Clojure) — no type at all, and no distinct concept.** "`fn` returns a function object" and "An anonymous function is a function without a name" (https://clojure.org/guides/higher_order_functions). A branch body is `#(= :dog (:type %))` — a value, passed, applied. **Evidence — and the sharpest single datum for the owner's question:** the loop in the same guide is not a function value at all. It is a macro-expanded named loop with a binding vector and a jump: `(loop [pets pets dogs []] (if (first pets) (recur (rest pets) …) dogs))` (same source). **Inference:** in the Lisp family the language gives control flow *its own construct* — `loop`/`recur` — rather than a type for the body. That is the opposite move from Khayyam's, and it is the only case examined where a language says no both to a function type *and* to a function value at the branch site.

**Haskell — no distinct concept; the type-level discipline is spent on the effect, not on the block.** `bracket :: IO a -> (a -> IO b) -> (a -> IO c) -> IO c`, `finally :: IO a -> IO b -> IO a`, `onException :: IO a -> IO b -> IO a`, and `catch :: Exception e => IO a -> (e -> IO a) -> IO a` (https://hackage.haskell.org/package/base/docs/Control-Exception.html). **Inference — the sharpest reading for Khayyam's type question:** every deferred body Haskell has is an ordinary argument of an ordinary function type; what the type system guarantees about the *order* in which those bodies run comes from the effect in the type (`IO a`), not from anything about the body. Khayyam's `sc` buys the same guarantee by a different route — the body is inert until a driver calls it (docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:129) — so the question "what is the type of a deferred block" has, in Haskell, the answer "nothing in particular; the interesting type is the one of the effect".

**D — the one language that names a `scope`, and it is not what `sc` is.** "RAII — Resource Acquisition can be provided by the `scope` storage class" (https://dlang.org/glossary.html). D's callable object is a named user type: "Functor — A user-defined type (struct or class) that defines the function call operator (opCall in D) so it can be used similarly to a function", and "Predicate — A function or delegate returning a Boolean result" (same source). **Inference:** this is a real counter-example to docs/protocols/process/control-flow.changelog.md:136's claim, and a weak one. D's `scope` is a *storage class on a declaration* — a lifetime annotation whose whole meaning is "this object is destroyed when the enclosing scope ends" — so the block is bound to a scope by *leaving* it, not by a library method calling it, and it is a property of an object's declaration rather than a type a signature can name. `sc`'s inertness (docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:129) is precisely the thing D does not have. The honest form of the prior-art claim is therefore narrower than the changelog's: *no language examined gives a deferred, inertly declared, named block a category of its own in the type system* — D names a scope but not that one.

**C — no distinct concept, no function type, and a jump. Added in the third pass.** The body is a *statement position*: `attr-spec-seq (optional) if ( expression ) statement-true`, where "statement-true — any statement (often a compound statement), which is executed if expression compares not equal to 0", and "the entire if-statement has its own block scope" (https://en.cppreference.com/w/c/language/if.html). There is no callable type anywhere in this; the mechanism is a transfer of control: "The `goto` statement causes an unconditional jump (transfer of control) to the statement prefixed by the named label", "Labels are the only identifiers that have function scope", and the conditional's behaviour under one is stated outright — "If statement-true is entered through a `goto`, statement-false is not executed" (https://en.cppreference.com/w/c/language/goto.html). **Inference:** C is the language that answers the owner's question (B) most bluntly. It has the block, it has the keyword, it gives the body no name and no type — and it pays for the absence of a function type by having its only non-local transfer be a jump to a labelled line. That is the mechanism Khayyam's grammar has no word for.

**JavaScript (ECMA-262) — no distinct concept; a function value whose body is a statement list.** Added in the third pass. The conditional's body is a **`Statement`**: `IfStatement[Yield, Await, Return] : if ( Expression [+In, ?Yield, ?Await] ) Statement [?Yield, ?Await, ?Return]` (https://tc39.es/ecma262/, §14.6). The runtime is in place and its mechanism is an abstract operation returning a *completion record*, not a jump: "Let `stmtCompletion` be Completion ( Evaluation of `Statement` )", "Let `exprValue` be `ToBoolean`(? `GetValue`( `exprRef` ))", and "If `exprValue` is `true`, then Let `stmtCompletion` be Completion ( Evaluation of the first `Statement` )" (same source). Where JavaScript *does* pass a body it passes a **function**, and that function's body is still statements: `ArrowFunction : ArrowParameters => ConciseBody`, `ConciseBody : ExpressionBody | { FunctionBody }`, `FunctionBody : FunctionStatementList` (same source, §15.3). **Inference:** this is the datum that sharpens question (B). JavaScript's arrow function is a value; its body is a statement list; the value and the body are different things. Being *the value your branch produces* — which is what a JavaScript or Rust `if` does, `if` being an expression there — is not being *the value your branch is*.

**Tcl — the decisive case, and it is a `tr`: a conditional that is not a keyword at all.** Added in the third pass, and this is the entry the previous two passes missed. "The `if` command evaluates `expr1` as an expression … if it is true then `body1` **is executed by passing it to the Tcl interpreter**. Otherwise `expr2` is evaluated … The `then` and `else` arguments are optional 'noise words' to make the command easier to read." and "The return value from the command is the result of the body script that was executed" (https://www.tcl-lang.org/man/tcl8.5/TclCmd/if.htm). **Evidence:** a conditional in Tcl has no keyword, no reserved word, and no place in the grammar's syntax categories; it is a builtin command, and its branch bodies are **arguments it is handed**. **Inference:** this is the design Khayyam has chosen — flow construct as a library operation, branch body as a named thing passed to it — and it is in wide use. What Tcl does *not* have is a way to *name* a branch body and put the call before it: the body is a script literal written as the command's argument, so it appears after the condition in the same command. That is the one difference, and it is what `sc` supplies.

**C++ — not verified.** cppreference returned 404 from this machine for both `named_req/Invocable` and `types/function` in the second pass, and the third pass did not retry them; no primary C++ source was fetched, and nothing about C++ is claimed here. **Note:** the third pass fetched cppreference's **C** pages successfully from the same host. The gap is a property of the pages, not of this machine's reach, and it is recorded because a reader could otherwise infer the opposite.

**Inference — what the languages jointly answer.** The decisive question was the same for each, and the answers cluster in four groups. (i) Go, Kotlin, Java, Clojure, Haskell, JavaScript: a *deferred* body is a function value wearing a hat; the function type exists and is written in the type position (`func`, `(A, B) -> C`, `'a -> 'b`); no language spends a *category* on the block. (ii) Rust: a function value wearing a hat, with a type-level bound hierarchy added because the captured environment is unwritten. (iii) Swift and Lisp-family: the language declines to use a value at the branch site and gives control flow its own construct. (iv) **C and Tcl:** no function type at all — C because its non-local transfer is a jump to a label, Tcl because its conditional is a command whose body is an argument it hands its own interpreter. D sits beside (ii) with a storage class. **No language examined puts the branch body in a Type category of its own** — which is what `docs/type.md:181-188` calls a Scope and what `sc` implements. **Inference:** that remains the single strongest piece of external evidence, and it cuts in favour of option (b) (keep `sc`, but let a signature name the category) rather than towards removal, because it says the category is the *unusual* choice and unusualness is something the owner should be told about — not that it is wrong. **What the third pass adds is group (iv)'s significance:** C and Tcl both work without a function type for the branch, and they work by *not needing* one — C because a jump needs no callee, Tcl because the callee is the interpreter and the body is a script. A Khayyam conditional needs a callee, so it needs a type for the callee, and there it is unlike both.

### What would play the role of the function type in Khayyam
**Evidence.** The language has no primitive types (`docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:206`: "Khayyam does not have primitive types. Even values that other languages represent as `int`, `bool`, or `string` are wrapped in named capsules"), no literal (docs/khayyam/targets/js/target.md:82: "The language has no primitive types, no literal, and no construction form … so every value a program handles was produced outside it"), and no construction form (modules/process/construction/construction.md:18 and modules/khayyam/targets/js/target.handoff.md:174). A signature writes *types*: `(influencing variables...)`, each entry a name and a type (`modules/khayyam/core/src/body.ts:121-138` reads an argument list as a list of type references). A type is one of four categories (`docs/type.md:191`).

**Inference.** Three candidates, and the evidence ranks them.
1. **The category keyword itself, written in a type position** — `(branch sc)`. The position is already admitted (docs/khayyam/method.md:71: "A method's influencing group may receive a `vr`, an `sc`, or an `mt`"), and modules/khayyam/targets/js/language-gaps.md:149-158 already names the missing term ("*Category Name* — a name of one of the four Type categories written where a signature writes a type, admitting every value of that category rather than the instances of one declared type"). Cost: the frontend must accept a keyword where it reads a name — a new accept row and possibly a new refusal label (modules/khayyam/targets/js/language-gaps.md:164).
2. **An `mt` in the position** — `(branch Body)`. Admitted by docs/khayyam/method.md:71 and already written by modules/khayyam/targets/js/bootstrap/behaviour.kh:15, and refused `unbound-type-name` because no unit declares a type that holds a method (modules/khayyam/targets/js/target.handoff.md:106, :174). Cost: the same new accept row, plus a decision about how a bare name writes a method value, which no document states (modules/khayyam/targets/js/language-gaps.md:27).
3. **A named abstraction** — `(branch BranchBody)`, satisfied by a named capsule per branch site, which is what docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md:95 already prescribes for every callback-shaped requirement ("There is no closure or lambda syntax in the grammar; any callback-shaped requirement is expressed as a named capsule implementing the relevant abstraction"). Cost: a declaration per branch site, and the substitution cost modules/khayyam/targets/js/sc-in-signature.md:47-51 names.

**Inference — the constraint that decides between 1 and 2.** A method in Khayyam cannot capture anything: there is no closure syntax (docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md:95) and a method's inputs are its influencing group (`docs/khayyam/method.md:39`). So a branch body written as a method reaches only what the driver passes it, and the driver's signature must name every one of those variables. **Inference:** options (c) and (d) converge on the same shape for exactly this reason — a method-as-branch-body and a capsule-as-branch-body differ in whether the body has fields or parameters, not in what the call site looks like. That is why docs/protocols/process/control-flow.changelog.md:124's reason ("losing the semantic distinction between 'a data capsule' and a control-flow scope") is a real loss and not a rhetorical one: under (c) and (d) the branch body *is* a data capsule, and the loss is precisely the reader's ability to tell a "run this later" body from a "this is the thing's state" object by looking at the declaration.

### The options, and what each one is
Five options. Each is stated with what it *is* — what mechanism performs flow management under it, what a reader has to hold in mind, and what the language can then express — and with the evidence that bears on it. The number of files a change would touch is **not** among the reasons; [the measurement of what a removal would touch](#what-a-removal-would-touch--a-measurement-not-an-argument) is reported separately and deliberately load-bearing for nothing.

**(a) Keep `sc` as it is.** What it is: the branch body is a named, inertly declared block in the method that owns the state, and a library method drives it. Grammar, compiler, rules, protocols and sources: untouched. Reader: a reader must understand one construct no other language has, and must understand that it is inert (docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:129). **Evidence favours this weakly.** The reach a branch body has over the enclosing method's names is a property of the construct itself, not of what a signature may write about it, so (a) keeps everything the language can do and leaves the spelling of the position where it stood. **Evidence against, weakly** — docs/protocols/process/control-flow.changelog.md:136's prior-art claim is the reason `sc` exists as a category rather than a keyword, and that claim is now partly false, so the strongest stated justification for the design is weaker than it was.

**(b) Keep `sc` and let a signature name the category the language declares.** Grammar: the position is already admitted (docs/khayyam/method.md:71); one spelling is missing. Compiler: one accept row in `core/` and possibly one refusal label (modules/khayyam/targets/js/language-gaps.md:164); the `scope` command and its lowering survive unchanged. Rules: modules/khayyam/rules/type-as-argument/type-as-argument.md:15 already says type-level arguments "that the signature explicitly allows remain valid", so the rule extends rather than changes. Protocols: modules/khayyam/targets/js/sc-in-signature.md's drafts D2 and D3 are exactly this, already written, and the owner may phrase them (that document's own judgment at `:141` and `:164`). Sources: nothing. Reader: a reader must learn that a signature may name a category rather than a type. **Evidence favours this most strongly of the five.** It keeps the one construct in the grammar that can perform a branch, and it answers the type question the way every language examined that has a function type answers it — by naming the thing in the type position — which makes the four-level stake (grammar, compiler, protocols, self-documenting) consistent instead of four separate positions asking for it. **What counts against it:** it does not resolve the abstraction-owned half (docs/khayyam/targets/js/sc-in-signature.md:31-33), and the owner's preference for an abstraction rather than the category is not this option.

**(b′) Keep `sc`, and type it as a named abstraction the language declares** — the owner's stated preference. Grammar: the position is admitted and a capsule may be named in a capsule-owned signature (docs/khayyam/abstraction.md:110); an abstraction-owned signature may name abstractions only (`docs/khayyam/abstraction.md:100`). Compiler: nothing new. Rules: nothing. Protocols: docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:37 and `:59` survive, since both are permissive. Sources: nothing. Reader: a reader must know that a declared abstraction stands for "any scope", which no document would say. **Inference:** this option needs one sentence no document carries — what relates a declared abstraction to the `sc` the grammar produces (docs/khayyam/targets/js/sc-in-signature.md:47-51, D4) — and it buys substitutability back, which is the one thing option (d) costs. **Evidence favours it second**, and it is the only option under which an organization can mandate its own control-flow library through the Linter (docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:130; modules/process/control-flow/answers.md:215).

**(c) Remove `sc`; express a branch body as a method handed to a method.** Grammar: nothing new is *admitted* (docs/khayyam/method.md:71 already admits an `mt`), but the position needs a spelling that does not exist. Compiler: `readScope` and the `scope` command go; `readCommand`'s `tp` dispatch (core/src/body.ts:200) must refuse; the two-pass binding lift that lets a driver name a scope declared below it (targets/js/src/emit.ts:46-49, :70-76) has no construct to serve, and **no document states that a body may name a method declared below it** — modules/khayyam/lsp/lsp.handoff.md:25 and docs/khayyam/khayyam.handoff.md:57 both record the two-pass reading as this toolchain's own. Rules: modules/khayyam/rules/scope-placement/ is deleted; type-as-argument's `sc` half is withdrawn. Protocols: control-flow's six example scopes become six method declarations, and its `:32`, `:34`, `:134` need rewriting; the Compiler protocol's `:35` and the Linter's "`sc`-as-common-denominator" (docs/protocols/computer/linter.changelog.md:17) both lose their subject. Sources: 2 probe declarations deleted, 4 comment lines stale. Reader: a reader must understand that a branch body is a method, that it cannot capture anything, and therefore that every name its body reaches is a parameter of the driver's signature. **Inference — and this is the finding that makes (c) weaker than it looks:** a method-as-branch-body cannot capture, so the branch's environment must be passed as parameters; that is the same shape as a capsule per branch, so (c) buys no reader-facing distinction over (d) while losing the reader the `sc`-is-inert fact that made the body recognisable. **Evidence favours it least of the five**, and the reason is the one [How flow management is performed](#how-flow-management-is-performed-and-what-would-perform-it-without-sc) gives: under (c) the driver cannot jump, and a `return` written inside the branch leaves the branch rather than the enclosing method, so the mechanism the owner asked about has to be an ordinary call returning a value that the caller then inspects.

**(d) Remove `sc`; express a branch body as a capsule instance.** Grammar: nothing new (a capsule may be named where a capsule is expected). Compiler: `readScope` and the `scope` command go, as in (c). Rules: as in (c). Protocols: as in (c). Sources: as in (c). Reader: a reader must understand that a named capsule is being used as a continuation, which nothing in the declaration says. **Evidence — a conflict no document records, found by cross-reading this research:** the tree's only measurement of this shape writes two signatures the language forbids. `modules/khayyam/targets/js/test/emit.test.ts:29` is `tp IF_OnTrue mt (self IF) (condition Condition, branch Scope) ()` and `:33` is `tp ELSE_OnFalse mt (self ELSE) (condition Condition, branch Scope) ()` — both owner-is-an-abstraction, both parameter-is-a-capsule — while `docs/khayyam/abstraction.md:100` refuses a capsule type in an abstraction's method signature. `modules/khayyam/targets/js/sc-in-signature.md:88` reads this fixture's shape as "the shape the ruling sanctions" and `:40-41` correctly notes that an abstraction's signature may name abstractions only, but neither states that the two abstraction-owned fixture signatures are therefore refused by the rule as written. **Inference:** under the rule as it stands, option (d) **cannot** realize an abstraction-owned conditional pair at all, which pushes the pair onto a concrete capsule and with it onto the loss docs/protocols/process/control-flow.changelog.md:124 describes. **Inference:** this makes (d) a decision about the forms' owner, not about the branch body's type, and it is therefore not a real alternative to (c) so much as its capsule-shaped form.

**Foreclosed by rulings already made.** The owner's ruling of 2026-10-02 — an Abstraction has no need of `sc`, a code scope is in essence a kind of implementation — forecloses any option in which a scope may be written where an abstraction writes its types (modules/khayyam/targets/js/sc-in-signature.md:26-33). It does **not** touch the grammar, the category, the capsule option, or the method option. **Foreclosed by a document.** The Grammar Refuses Protocol Semantics (docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:169-174) forecloses writing control-flow *semantics* into the grammar, not the grouping primitive. Minimal Legislation (`:176-179`) sends *placement* to a rule, which is why the placement rule exists and why its removal is a separate decision from `sc`'s. Nothing in the sources foreclose anything.

### The question asked is two questions
**Inference, and this is the research's own product.** "Do we need the concept of a code scope?" conflates two claims that the evidence bears on differently.
- **Q-grammar:** does *Khayyam's grammar* need a named, inertly declared, block-valued construct that a library method drives? Evidence: the needs in the sources nothing; the compiler's command set would lose its only body-level declaration; Swift and the Lisp family show that a language which declines to use a value at the branch site has to introduce a *different* construct there, and Swift's `defer` and Clojure's `loop`/`recur` are what that looks like; Haskell shows the alternative — make the block an ordinary function argument and put the discipline in the effect type. Nothing found here compels `sc`, and nothing found here forbids it.
- **Q-category:** does *the Memar base layer* need a Scope as one of its four Type categories? Evidence: `docs/type.md:184` names five realizations of the category, of which `sc` is one; `:186` places placement with the realization; `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:174` says the grammar expresses the base concept, not that it is the base concept's only expression. **Inference:** removing `sc` does not by itself refute the category, but it would leave the category with no realization in the language that motivates it — and `docs/type.md:188` makes the category's standing conditional on the construct's semantic significance, so the base layer would need a sentence it does not have. **This is the part of the question that is genuinely open, and it is a base-layer question, not a grammar question.**

## Addendum — the ruling, the closure finding, and what the cost argument was not
**Owner ruling, 2026-10-02.** The code scope stays. The ground given is what the language can express, not what removal would cost: "as far as I can see `mt` cannot cover many of the details." The ruling also rejects the method this record's ranking had drifted into — a decision may not be argued from the number of files that would have to change. Two questions this record had carried close with the ruling are closed by it rather than left open: `docs/type.md:198`'s single Scope relation row keeps its subject, because the realization it names stays; and `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:145`'s claim that lifetime correctness comes from five sources, one of which is deterministic `sc` boundaries, stands as written.

**The hypothesis, tested.** The hypothesis put to the compiler was: *a code scope closes over the enclosing method's variables, and a method declaration cannot.* **Measurement — it holds.** In targets/js/src/emit.ts (`bundles/khayyam/targets/js/src/emit.ts`), `bind` at `:51-85` builds one root block per method: the owner parameter and every parameter of both groups go into it at `:52-63`, every `vr` the body declares is added to the block that declares it at `:66-69`, and every `sc` the body declares is added to the enclosing block at `:70-79` — the nested block it opens at `:71` is a fresh `emptyBlock()` holding only what is written inside it. The cells and scopes of a block are lifted out of command order by the same walk, which is what makes a forward reference work. Resolution then runs over the whole chain rather than the innermost block: `findLocal` at `:166-172` walks every block it is given, and `eachInvocation` at `:267-281` hands each nested scope the chain plus its own block (`:279`), so the chain of a scope's block contains every block between it and the method body. A name a scope's block uses is therefore looked up in the enclosing method's cells and scopes first and only then falls outside them.

**Proof.** Eleven programs were compiled and emitted through `compileProgram` and `emitUnit` in a throwaway harness under the system temp directory, deleted afterwards; the programs are written out below so they can be added as a probe under `bundles/khayyam/targets/js/bootstrap/` and run by `bundles/khayyam/targets/js/tests/bootstrap.test.ts` beside the probes already there. Seven were accepted and four refused. What each acceptance measured:

```khayyam
// Probe A — a scope's block reaches the enclosing method's `vr` and its parameter.
tp Run mt (self Text) (seed Text) () {
    vr local Text
    tp Branch sc {
        self.Emit(local, seed)()
    }
    self.Drive(Branch)()
}
```
Emitted: `function $Branch$() { Text$Emit(self, local$, seed$); }`, declared inside `Text$Run` (`emit.ts:423-429`), with `local$` a cell the enclosing function holds (`emit.ts:421`). **Accepted.**

```khayyam
// Probe B — two levels of nesting, reaching the method's influenced parameter.
tp Run mt (self Text) () (out Text) {
    tp Outer sc {
        tp Inner sc {
            self.Emit(out)()
        }
        self.Emit(Inner)()
    }
    self.Emit(Outer)()
}
```
Emitted: `function $Outer$() { function $Inner$() { Text$Emit(self, out$); } … }`. **Accepted.**

```khayyam
// Probe C — the enclosing method's `vr`, declared *below* the scope.
tp Run mt (self Text) () (unused Text) {
    tp Branch sc {
        self.Emit(later)()
    }
    vr later Text
    self.Drive(Branch)()
}
```
Emitted with `later$` hoisted above `$Branch$`. **Accepted.**

```khayyam
// Probe D — a call *inside* the scope writes the enclosing method's `vr` through it.
tp Run mt (self Text) () (unused Text) {
    vr local Text
    tp Branch sc {
        self.Take()(local)
    }
    self.Drive(Branch)()
}
```
Emitted: `function $Branch$() { Text$Take(self, local$); }` — the enclosing cell is handed over by reference from inside the block. **Accepted.** A scope's block therefore reaches the enclosing method's variables for reading and for writing alike.

Three further acceptances complete the picture: a scope's block reads the owner's own field as `self.field` and calls on it (`Text$Emit(self.state.value)`); a scope's block names a type marker of the unit (`Text.Emit()()`); and a method declared at file level names a type marker (`Text.Emit()()` in a body of its own). The last two are the same shape as a `sc` block's and are accepted for the same reason — a name nothing binds falls to a type the program exports — which is what makes the contrast sharp: a file-level method has that fallback and a `vr` does not.

The contrasts, each of which the same harness refused: a scope's own `vr` is not reachable from a sibling scope (`inner: no type of this unit or of one it includes is named so`); a scope's name is not reachable from another method's body (`Only: no type of this unit or of one it includes is named so`); and **a method declared at file level cannot see another method's local at all** — `tp Stray mt (self Text) (seed Text) () { self.Emit(local)() }` against a `local` declared in a sibling method's body is refused with `local: no type of this unit or of one it includes is named so`. `bind` seeds a method's root block with its own parameters and nothing else (`emit.ts:52-63`), and a name it does not bind falls to its owner's own fields (`fieldOf`, `:188-201`) and then to a type the program exports (`markerOf`, `:128-139`) before being refused (`emit.ts:243-257`). A file-level `vr` is accepted by the frontend and reachable from no method body at all.

**Inference.** The asymmetry is real and it is the whole of what a branch body gets. A `sc` written in a method body reads and writes that method's variables without naming them in any signature; a method declared at file level has no enclosing body to close over, so every name its body touches must be a parameter of its own signature — and therefore a parameter of the driver that hands it over. A branch body written as a method therefore forces the enclosing method's locals to be written a second time, in the driver's signature, at every branch site. Nothing else in the language has this reach: a capsule instance is handed its fields explicitly, and `encapsulation.md:95` refuses the anonymous-capture form outright. The frontend side agrees — `sc` is the one subtype a body admits (`core/src/body.ts:176-195`, dispatched from `:200`), and a scope inside a body is a command of that body rather than a declaration of the unit (`targets/js/src/program.ts:230-231`).

**Measurement — what the documents say.** Nothing. `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:126-131` gives the form, the inertness, the newline rule, and the type-level argument, and says nothing about what a scope's block may reach. `docs/khayyam/method.md` — read in full — says nothing about what any body may reach, for a scope or otherwise; it fixes the signature, pass-by-reference, and the three values a type-level argument may take (`:71`). `docs/type.md:181-188` describes the Scope category in terms of visibility, ownership, isolation, and entry/exit, names "a local algorithmic block (as used by `if`, `loop`, `goto`)" among five realizations (`:184`), places placement with the realization rather than the category (`:186`), makes the category's standing conditional on the construct's semantic significance (`:188`), and gives Scope's one cross-category relation as "commonly used within | Scope → Method | current Khayyam usage pattern, not a containment requirement" (`:198`) — never what the block resolves names from. `modules/khayyam/rules/scope-placement/scope-placement.md` speaks only of where a `sc` may be written (`:12`) and disclaims any claim about the language (`:20-23`); it is silent on reach too. So the compiler's behaviour is not forbidden by any document, and it is not stated by any document. Under [`Khayyam → Minimal Legislation: A Principle`](../../docs/khayyam/khayyam.md#minimal-legislation-a-principle) (`:177`) a construct earns its place in the grammar when a program's *meaning* turns on it — and that section's closing paragraph (`:181`) names what the grammar owes a program as "the declarations a program may contain, the meanings those declarations have" — so the reach is the grammar's to state and no document states it. That is the finding: **the language has a capability no document says it has.**

**The one document that touches capture argues against it.** `docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md:93` defines a closure structurally — "whatever a closure captures is, in effect, a set of unnamed fields" — and `:95` states flatly that "there is no closure or lambda syntax in the grammar," which the compiler honours: a `sc` block is a block in the source, not a function expression. But `:99` gives the reason for the refusal as "hidden state capturing creates invisible dependencies and breaks explicit state management", and a scope's reach is not hidden — every name it reaches is written in the enclosing method, in ordinary source, and every name it writes is written through a named slot. So the *reason* does not reach this case and the *statement* does. Which sentence survives is the owner's; that a sentence is owed is not.

**How this sits against the type argument, honestly. — The next two paragraphs are the second pass's reasoning and are partly withdrawn by the third; they are kept as the record of what was argued, and [Recommendation](#recommendation) says what stands.** The research's own strongest external evidence was about types: no language examined gives a deferred block a Type category of its own, so `sc`'s category is the unusual choice. That claim stands; the addendum does not withdraw it. But it is a comparative claim about other languages' type systems, while the closure finding is an intrinsic claim about what this construct does inside this language, and the second pass treated the second as the better ground — because the first is downstream of the second. The chain ran: a branch body needs the enclosing method's variables; nothing else in the language gives it that reach; so it cannot be an ordinary method or an ordinary capsule; so it needs a declaration form of its own; and a declaration form that a signature may name needs a category to be named as. **The third pass withdraws the premise, not the conclusion:** reach *is* ordinary, so "nothing else in the language gives it that reach" is true only within Khayyam and is not what makes the construct necessary. What survives is narrower and is stated at [Recommendation](#recommendation) — what a branch body needs is a *name in an argument position*, and reach is only one of the three reasons the name is load-bearing. The research's own Swift entry (`:113`) is the same fact seen from outside — Swift reaches for a separate *statement* because "a function value has no lexical attachment to the scope it was written in" — and that observation survives this pass intact, because Swift's `defer` is a keyword whose body is not handed to anything.

**What the cost argument was, removed.** Two rankings in [The options, and what each one is](#the-options-and-what-each-one-is) rested on the repository rather than on the language, and both are corrected in place above. Option (a) was favoured because it was "the only option under which nothing in the tree moves, and the sources already compiles"; option (b) was favoured most strongly because it was "the only option that (i) leaves every other thing in the tree alone" among its four reasons. Neither reason survives the ruling: whether a decision is *right* cannot depend on how many files were written before it was taken. The section [What a removal would touch](#what-a-removal-would-touch--a-measurement-not-an-argument) already declared itself "a cost statement, not an argument" at `:153` and remains a measurement; it is no longer load-bearing for anything, and the recommendation below no longer rests on it. One cost is not the repository's and is not withdrawn: the *reader* of a Khayyam program must understand one construct no other language has, and must understand that it is inert (`docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:129`). That is a property of the language as designed, it is paid by anyone who writes or reads Khayyam, and it is the kind of cost the ruling leaves standing.

**Does the recommendation survive with cost set to zero? Yes, and it changes shape rather than direction. — Partly superseded by the third pass.** With repository cost at zero the second pass's ordering was still (b) or (b′) over (c) or (d), on the closure ground. **The third pass withdraws that ground** — reach is ordinary, and the owner was right about that — and replaces it with the narrower claim at [Recommendation](#recommendation): the concept exists because the conditional is a library method and a library method's argument must be a named, typed thing. What survives of this paragraph is the shape of its finding, which the third pass confirms for the first time in a language that did the same thing: a `sc`-less Khayyam writes the enclosing method's locals a second time in the driver's signature, at every branch site, and a driver asked to decide *when* to run a body is thereby made to know *what* the body does with the method's state.

**Researcher's own view, as first written.** Superseded by [Recommendation](#recommendation), which keeps the direction and replaces the ground. The paragraph is left because its reversal condition is the one thing the later evidence does not touch: *if the language admitted a first-class function value that carried a lexical attachment to the block it was written in, then `sc` would be one spelling of that value rather than the only carrier of the reach, its separate category would earn nothing, and the case for it would rest on the reader's convenience alone.* The language has no such value and refuses the anonymous form (`docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md:95`), so today nothing else can hold the role.

## How flow management is performed, and what would perform it without `sc`
The owner's first question, and the one no version of this record answered from the right place: not what a removal would touch, but **what actually takes one branch instead of the other**. The second pass answered it by reasoning about what the grammar could express. The third pass answers it by reading what the compiler emits and what the link phase is supposed to supply — and the answer is not what the second pass said.

### What the toolchain this repository ships actually does
**Measurement — the body becomes a function, and is handed over as a value.** `modules/khayyam/targets/js/src/emit.ts:423-429` emits a `sc` as a nested `function $Name$() { … }` declared inside the method that holds it, over the enclosing method's cells — which `emit.ts:418-422` has already hoisted as `const name$ = { value: undefined }` in the enclosing function, so the emitted function closes over them. A scope is a `Local` of its own kind (`emit.ts:26`, `kind: "scope"`), and `valueOf` / `referenceOf` hand it to a call as `$Name$` (`emit.ts:342-346`, `:354-358`).

**Measurement — and that is exactly the argument position.** `bind` at `emit.ts:73-76` puts the scope into its block's `cells` under the comment that states the reason: "A scope is a name of the body like any other: a call names it as its own argument, and a scope is what an argument position admits". So `sc` is **not** a construct the driver reaches by some private channel: it is written where an argument goes, and the argument is what the driver receives.

**Measurement — the driver receives it and passes it straight on.** The tree's only end-to-end measurement of a driver is `modules/khayyam/targets/js/test/emit.test.ts:35-43`:

```typescript
tp OnTrue mt (self Flow) (condition Condition, branch Scope) () {
    self.Drive(condition, branch)()
}
```

`branch` is a parameter of the driver, and `OnTrue` does nothing with it but hand it to `Drive`. The built library shows the same shape at the emitted level — `modules/khayyam/targets/js/build/modules/khayyam/targets/js/lib/control-flow.js:32-36` packs `branch` into a slot and calls `Flow$Drive(self, condition$.value, branch$.value)` — and `control-flow.js:24-26` is `Flow$Drive`, which has no body and forwards to `$supplied["Flow$Drive"]`, populated at link time (`control-flow.js:7-12`; `docs/khayyam/method.md:77` and `:80` — a body-less method whose receiver is a concrete capsule "signals to the compiler that the implementation will be provided externally during the linking phase").

**Measurement — and the deciding half does not exist.** `modules/khayyam/targets/js/link_map.json` is `{}`. A search of `modules/` and `docs/` for `Flow$Drive`, `Flow.Drive` and `.Drive(` returns the fixture above and nothing else. **So in this repository the branch body is specified, typed, lowered, passed and forwarded, and the thing that decides to call it is written nowhere.**

**Inference — the answer to the owner's question, in one sentence.** The mechanism that takes one branch instead of the other in Khayyam is **an ordinary call, made by an ordinary library method, to the value it was handed at an argument position** — not a jump, not a branch table, not a returned continuation, not an interpreter frame. A `sc` supplies the callee; the driver supplies the decision; and the decision is ordinary code in the driver's own body.

**Inference — and this corrects two sentences in the language documents.** `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:130` says "a compiler of this language lowers `sc`-driven branches to internal jumps", and `docs/protocols/computer/compiler.md:35` repeats that "the compiler of a Khayyam realization lowers scope-driven branches to internal jumps". **The compiler this repository ships does not do that**: it emits a call (`emit.ts:423-429`, and the emitted `Flow$OnTrue`). **Inference:** the documented jump is nonetheless reachable without colliding with `compiler.md:33`, because inlining a call whose callee the compiler already knows requires recognising no library *name* — it is an ordinary optimization, available to any language with known callees. So the sentence is defensible as a claim about an optimizing realization and false as a claim about the one in the tree. It is the owner's to correct; this record states the conflict and does not edit the document.

**Measurement — and a second sentence is contradicted by the emitted code.** `emit.ts:431-434` emits a `return` command as `return;` inside whatever function the surrounding block became. For a `return` written inside a `sc`, that function is `$Name$`. **So a `return` inside a branch leaves the branch, not the enclosing method — in the toolchain this repository ships.** The previous version of this record asserted the opposite ("a `return` inside a branch can leave the enclosing method today") in two places; both statements are withdrawn. What `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:130` offers for mid-scope exit is that a mid-scope return "is an IR marker", and `docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:34` says "A mid-scope return operation is therefore ordinary control flow" — but no document says what it returns *from*, and the emitted code says it returns from the scope.

### The mechanisms, by language, from the source that states each
Each entry names what the specification says the construct **is**, and the mechanism is what that statement requires. Where a specification describes its constructs through abstract operations rather than through control transfers, that is said rather than smoothed over; where a specification only places the body in a production, the machine-level lowering is **not claimed**.

**A jump.** C: "The `goto` statement causes an unconditional jump (transfer of control) to the statement prefixed by the named label", and the conditional's behaviour under one is spelled out — "If `statement-true` is entered through a `goto`, `statement-false` is not executed" (https://en.cppreference.com/w/c/language/goto.html). Go: "A `goto` statement transfers control to the statement with the corresponding label within the same function", and "A `continue` statement begins the next iteration of the innermost enclosing `for` loop by advancing control to the end of the loop block" (https://go.dev/ref/spec). Go also pays for the jump with a rule about what it may cross: "Executing the `goto` statement must not cause any variables to come into scope that were not already in scope at the point of the `goto`" (same source).

**A construct evaluated in place, mechanism unspecified below the specification.** Go: `IfStmt = "if" [ SimpleStmt ";" ] Expression Block [ "else" ( IfStmt | Block ) ]` (same source). Rust: `IfExpression → if Conditions BlockExpressionNoInnerAttributes ( else ( BlockExpressionNoInnerAttributes | IfExpression ) )?`, and what selects the arm is stated as "the consequent block is executed and any subsequent else if or else block is skipped" (https://doc.rust-lang.org/reference/expressions/if-expr.html). **Inference:** both describe the selecting as part of the construct's own semantics and say nothing about a transfer; no claim is made here about what either compiler emits.

**An interpreter step.** Python is the one language examined whose reference manual describes the frames themselves: "A code block is executed in an execution frame. A frame contains some administrative information (used for debugging) and **determines where and how execution continues after the code block's execution has completed**" (https://docs.python.org/3/reference/executionmodel.html, second pass's source), with the selecting stated at the construct: "It selects exactly one of the suites by evaluating the expressions one by one until one is found to be true; then that suite is executed (and no other part of the `if` statement is executed or evaluated)" (https://docs.python.org/3/reference/compound_stmts.html §8.1).

**An interpreter step with a completion record.** JavaScript: the mechanism is the abstract operation `Evaluation` and the value it returns is a completion — "Let `stmtCompletion` be Completion ( Evaluation of `Statement` )" (https://tc39.es/ecma262/, §14.6.2). **Inference:** leaving a block early in JavaScript is a completion record propagating, and the language's `break` / `continue` / `return` are the writers of such records; that is a different mechanism from a jump, and it is the one the specification states.

**A call to a function value.** Haskell (second pass's sources): `when :: Applicative f => Bool -> f () -> f ()`, `guard :: Alternative f => Bool -> f ()`, `bracket :: IO a -> (a -> IO b) -> (a -> IO c) -> IO c`, `catch :: Exception e => IO a -> (e -> IO a) -> IO a`. The condition and the body are ordinary arguments and the body is an ordinary value; what guarantees the order is the effect type, not the block.

**A command invocation.** Tcl (third pass): "if it is true then `body1` **is executed by passing it to the Tcl interpreter**" (https://www.tcl-lang.org/man/tcl8.5/TclCmd/if.htm). The conditional is a builtin; the body is one of its arguments; the interpreter is the machine.

**A returned continuation — claimed by nobody.** **Inference:** no specification fetched in this research states that a conditional hands its driver a value to call later, so none is claimed for any language. This is also why Khayyam's own `A4` below is not a mechanism any surveyed language reports using.

### What that leaves for Khayyam
**Inference — the constraint that decides the shape, restated for a reader who has just read the mechanism.** `docs/protocols/computer/compiler.md:33` reads in full: "The compiler's front end accepts the constructs the language specification actually defines. It does not treat library-provided operations as intrinsics because they are common, useful, or imported from a privileged package. **Framework control-flow methods, numeric helpers, and lifecycle hooks are ordinary library code.** Special-casing any of them by name couples the compiler to one library and forces every other library through that library's shape." A Khayyam conditional is therefore ordinary library code in the compiler's eyes, and the driver has to be written as ordinary code too.

**Inference — the one-sentence answer, now that the mechanism is known.** With `sc` gone, **there is nothing at the argument position for a driver to receive.** Not "nothing can jump" — that was the second pass's phrasing and it was beside the point, because the mechanism was never a jump. The precise fact is that the mechanism is a call to a value the program must supply, and the only things Khayyam can declare are a variable, a scope, or a method. A variable is not callable. A method can be, but not by a spelling the language has. **So the `sc`-less language has no branch body at all, and a conditional degenerates into "call something, look at what came back, call the next thing".**

**Inference — the honest options, and what each requires the language to be able to do.**

**A1 — the branch is a method, the driver calls it. This is the option the owner named.** Mechanism: an ordinary call, which is what the mechanism already is. Requires three things of the language, none of which it has. (i) *Writing a method as a value*: the position is admitted (`docs/khayyam/method.md:71` — "A method's influencing group may receive a `vr`, an `sc`, or an `mt`") and no spelling exists, which is why `modules/khayyam/targets/js/bootstrap/behaviour.kh:15` is refused `unbound-type-name`. (ii) *The branch reaching the enclosing method's variables*: it cannot — `bind` seeds a method's root block with its own parameters and nothing else (`emit.ts:52-63`), so a file-level method reaches no sibling method's local at all (the Addendum's probe was refused). Every name the branch touches must therefore be a parameter of the driver's influencing group, written a second time at every branch site. (iii) *A type for the argument*: `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:131` and `docs/khayyam/method.md:71` admit `sc` and `mt` in that position by name; with `sc` gone only `mt` remains, and no document says what a method value's type is or how a bare name writes one. **Inference:** A1 is a real option and the owner was right to name it; it is also the option this research has called (c) since the first pass, and it needs three capabilities of the language rather than the file edits its name suggests.

**A2 — the branch is a named capsule, the driver calls it.** Mechanism: the same ordinary call, on a receiver. Requires everything A1 requires, plus a declared capsule type per branch site, plus a way past `docs/khayyam/abstraction.md:100` where the driver is owned by an abstraction.

**A3 — an explicit state machine; no branch body at all.** Mechanism: an ordinary call returning a state capsule, and an ordinary inspection of that state. Requires **nothing the grammar does not already have** — `vr`, an invocation, and `return` are sufficient (`modules/khayyam/core/src/body.ts:13-23`, minus the `scope` arm) — so this is the only branch-free option implementable today with no grammar change whatsoever. The shape, written in the target's `.kh` dialect to be concrete rather than to be adopted (**Inference — the shape, not a design**):

```khayyam
tp Decide mt (self Condition) (step Step) () {
    // no branch body: the decision is which step to run next
    vr next Step
    condition.NextStep(step)(next)
    Step.Run(next)()
}
```

**Inference:** the protocol survives this intact — `docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:34`'s separation of *what* would run from *when* it runs is untouched, because the state capsule names the *what* and the driver owns the *when*. **What it costs the language as designed:** a conditional is no longer written where it is read; the body is written once today (Addendum, Probe A and Probe D) and once per step without `sc`; the reader has to hold a state machine to read what is one branch today; and the protocol sentence at `docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:32` — that the branch-grouping primitive is the common denominator across realizations — loses its subject in this realization and the protocol would need one sentence saying what the common denominator is when a realization has none.

**A4 — the body yields a continuation to a driver loop.** Mechanism: a returned closure. Requires a value of function type produced from a body, which is a closure — and `docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md:95` states "There is no closure or lambda syntax in the grammar", while `docs/khayyam/metaprogramming.md:48` forecloses the neighbouring escape ("no compile-time meta-programming/code-generation facility baked into the language itself"). **Foreclosed by a document.** And note that this option was listed first in the second pass's version of this section without asking whether any surveyed language reports using it: **none does** — see *A returned continuation — claimed by nobody* above.

**A5 — a new grammar keyword for the jump or the conditional.** **Foreclosed by the owner's own position** that control flow is a protocol above the syntax (`docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:32`, `:127`) and by the alternatives the protocol's changelog already declined (`docs/protocols/process/control-flow.changelog.md:123`, `:131`).

**Inference — the shape of the answer.** Of the four that remain, only A3 needs nothing new, and it is the one that changes what a reader reads. A1 and A2 need a spelling the grammar lacks, a type for the argument, and a reach a file-level method cannot have; A4 and A5 are foreclosed. **This is a mechanism argument, and it holds with every file in this repository deleted.** One further measurement belongs here as an honest counterweight: **the decision half is not implemented today either** — `Drive` is a body-less method with no link entry. So "flow management without `sc`" is not a retreat from a working design; it is a retreat from a half-built one, in either direction, and the owner should weigh it as such.

## Why other languages have a code scope in `if`, and what the one that dropped its keyword pays
The owner's second question, and the one the first version answered only about types and the second version answered wrongly. **The answer is not about branching at all, and it is not about types. It is about where the conditional lives.** In a language whose conditional is a keyword, the grammar's production already has a slot for the body, so the body needs no name, no type and no value. In a language whose conditional is a library method, the body is an argument, and an argument must be a name of a declared type. `sc` exists because Khayyam's conditional is a library method.

### What `if`'s body *is*, in the languages the owner named, plus two more
**Evidence — five specifications, each quoting its own production.** C: `attr-spec-seq (optional) if ( expression ) statement-true`, where "statement-true — **any statement (often a compound statement)**, which is executed if `expression` compares not equal to 0"; and "As with all other selection and iteration statements, the entire if-statement has its own block scope" (https://en.cppreference.com/w/c/language/if.html). Go: `IfStmt = "if" [ SimpleStmt ";" ] Expression Block [ "else" ( IfStmt | Block ) ]` with `Block = "{" StatementList "}"` (https://go.dev/ref/spec). Rust: `IfExpression → if Conditions BlockExpressionNoInnerAttributes ( else ( BlockExpressionNoInnerAttributes | IfExpression ) )?` (https://doc.rust-lang.org/reference/expressions/if-expr.html). JavaScript: `IfStatement[Yield, Await, Return] : if ( Expression [+In, ?Yield, ?Await] ) Statement [?Yield, ?Await, ?Return]` (https://tc39.es/ecma262/ §14.6). Python: `if_stmt : "if" assignment_expression ":" suite ( "elif" assignment_expression ":" suite )* [ "else" ":" suite ]` (https://docs.python.org/3/reference/compound_stmts.html §8.1).

**Evidence — the answer to "is there a thing there at all?"** **No.** In all five the body is a *syntactic position whose content is statements* (C: a statement, where a bare statement needs no braces at all; JavaScript and Python: a statement list or a suite; Go and Rust: a block). Not one of the five gives that position a name, a type, or a value. **Inference:** so there is nothing in `if` for a "code scope" to be an instance of. What `if` has is a slot and a scoping rule, and the scoping rule is stated as one: Go's "Blocks nest and influence scoping" with "Each 'if', 'for', and 'switch' statement is considered to be in its own implicit block"; Python's "If the definition occurs in a function block, the scope extends to any blocks contained within the defining one"; C's "the entire if-statement has its own block scope" (the three sources above; Python's execution model for the second quotation).

**Inference — and this is the real answer to "could they not have said it is not needed?"** They did not need a *concept*, because they needed a *position*, and a syntactic position is free: the grammar is already writing one. **They also did not need the position to be named or typed, because nothing ever hands it to anybody.** A keyword conditional holds the body's code itself. That is the whole of it. Every one of the five would be unable to accept Khayyam's `IF(isValid, ValidData)` — not because a block is a poor idea, but because there is nowhere to *put* an argument in a production that already has a slot filled by the body.

### Which languages pass the body as a value, and which execute it in place
**Evidence — executed in place, from the sources just quoted.** C, Go, Rust, JavaScript, Python: the specification's own words are "executed", "is executed", "the consequent block is executed", "Completion ( Evaluation of Statement )", "that suite is executed". **Evidence — passed as a value.** Haskell: `when :: Applicative f => Bool -> f () -> f ()`, `guard :: Alternative f => Bool -> f ()`, `bracket :: IO a -> (a -> IO b) -> (a -> IO c) -> IO c`, `catch :: Exception e => IO a -> (e -> IO a) -> IO a` (second pass's sources), so the condition and the body are ordinary arguments and the body is an ordinary value. JavaScript, Go and Kotlin elsewhere: the body is wrapped at the call site — `ArrowFunction : ArrowParameters => ConciseBody`, `ConciseBody : ExpressionBody | { FunctionBody }`, `FunctionBody : FunctionStatementList` (ECMA-262 §15.3); Go's "`defer` … The expression must be a function or method call; it cannot be parenthesized"; Kotlin's "If the lambda is the only argument in that call, the parentheses can be omitted entirely". **Evidence — the conditional itself is neither:** Tcl, where `if` is a builtin command and "if it is true then `body1` is executed by passing it to the Tcl interpreter" (https://www.tcl-lang.org/man/tcl8.5/TclCmd/if.htm).

### Did any language say "a conditional body is just a function value", and what did it cost?
**Two corrections to what the second pass of this record said, stated plainly.** The second pass wrote: "a language that passes a conditional body **as a value** has no conditional statement to pass it to — it has a function you call. No language examined both kept a conditional statement and made its body a value, and none has successfully said 'a conditional body is just a function value' in a language that also has `if`." **That is wrong as stated and is withdrawn.** It conflates a language's *having* a conditional with a conditional's *body* being a value. Haskell has both an `if` and function values; so do JavaScript, Go, Kotlin, Clojure. The claim that survives is narrower and still useful: **no language examined lets the body of its `if` be a value.** What the nearest cases do is a different move — their `if` **evaluates to** a value, because it is an expression (Rust's `IfExpression`; JavaScript's `if` returning `?A UpdateEmpty( stmtCompletion, undefined )`), while the body remains a statement list. **Inference:** *being the value your branch produces is not being the value your branch is*, and that distinction is the entire reason a library-driven conditional needs a type for its branch argument.

**Evidence — the case that did it, from a primary source.** **Tcl's conditional is not a keyword at all.** "The `if` command evaluates `expr1` as an expression (in the same way that `expr` evaluates its argument) … if it is true then `body1` is executed by passing it to the Tcl interpreter." and "The `then` and `else` arguments are optional 'noise words' to make the command easier to read." (https://www.tcl-lang.org/man/tcl8.5/TclCmd/if.htm). **Inference:** so the answer to the owner's question — "could they not have said a conditional body is just a function value, did any of them, and what did it cost?" — is **yes, Tcl did it, and what it cost was the keyword, not the body.** `if` in Tcl has no reserved-word status, is brought into scope like any other command, and takes its bodies as arguments; its non-local exits (`break`, `continue`, `return`) are themselves commands that raise errors rather than jumps. **Inference:** and the thing Tcl does *not* have is a way to **name** a branch body and put the call before it — the body is a script literal written as the command's own argument. That single missing capability is what `sc` supplies, and it is a declaration, not a type.

**Evidence — the one experiment where the block itself was dropped, and its result.** "In the early days of computing, many languages such as FORTRAN IV and BASIC lacked block syntax except sometimes for rudimentary loop constructs. **A conditional was encoded using the goto statement.** In the following FORTRAN 66 code, the logical structure is much less clear due to the lack of blocks" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_(programming)). **Inference:** this remains the closest thing to a controlled answer, and it now sits beside two others. FORTRAN IV and BASIC dropped the block and got a jump to a labelled line, and the reader lost the structure. C kept the block, kept the keyword, and got the jump anyway. Tcl dropped the keyword and got the body-as-argument. **Inference:** the three cases together say the same thing from three sides — **no language examined found that "a function does the job just as well" for a conditional body.** Go, the one fetched language that kept both the block and the keyword, kept `goto` as well and paid for it with a rule about what a jump may cross: "Executing the `goto` statement must not cause any variables to come into scope that were not already in scope at the point of the `goto`" (https://go.dev/ref/spec).

### What this settles about `sc`
**Inference.** `sc` is a **named body in an argument position**. That is exactly what Tcl has, and exactly what a keyword language does not need. The chain is short and every link is a document or a measurement:

1. `docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:32` places flow constructs in the library: "its grammar ships no control-flow keywords or logical operators, and its control-flow abstractions are library-level method calls brought into scope explicitly via `in`."
2. `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:129` fixes the consequence: "A code scope is inert until a library-provided method drives it."
3. A library method's branch-body parameter must be a **name of a declared type** — `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:131` ("An argument position may be satisfied by a `vr` of the declared type or, for `sc` and `mt`, by the type itself passed as a type-level argument") and `docs/khayyam/method.md:71` ("A method's influencing group may receive a `vr`, an `sc`, or an `mt`").
4. A name must be **declared** somewhere, and the only place a program can declare a named thing that is not the enclosing method's own is a `sc` — `modules/khayyam/core/src/body.ts:13-23` (four command forms, of which `scope` is the only declaration) and `:200` (`tp` in a body dispatches to `readScope`).
5. **Measurement, the reach that follows from it:** `emit.ts:423-429` emits the `sc` as a function inside the method and `:418-422` hoists the method's cells for it to close over, so the branch body reaches the enclosing method's state — which a file-level method cannot (`emit.ts:52-63`; the Addendum's refusal).

**Inference — the code scope is therefore not a control-flow concept in this language. It is the declaration form that a library-driven conditional needs.** That is a different claim from the second pass's — that no language lets the body be a value, so `sc` cannot be a value — and it is a better one, because it explains the *one* language that did it without a block-level concept (Tcl), predicts what `sc` is for, and does not rest on a dichotomy this pass has had to withdraw half of.

## Whether a block's reach is exotic
The owner's third question, and **the owner is right: reach is ordinary.** The second pass's evidence for exoticness does not survive this pass, and what replaces it is a different finding rather than the absence of one. This section now settles the conflict question and answers "which of the two should the owner keep", which the second pass declined to answer.

**Evidence — reach is stated, everywhere, as a rule with edges.** Python: "If the definition occurs in a function block, the scope extends to any blocks contained within the defining one, unless a contained block introduces a different binding for the name", and its edge case is stated too — "The scope of names defined in a class block is limited to the class block; it does not extend to the code blocks of methods" — and reaching *outward* across a function boundary needs a declaration: "The `nonlocal` statement causes corresponding names to refer to previously bound variables in the nearest enclosing function scope" (https://docs.python.org/3/reference/executionmodel.html). Go: "Blocks nest and influence scoping" (https://go.dev/ref/spec). C: reach is function-wide and its exception is named the other way round — "Labels are the only identifiers that have function scope: they can be used (in a `goto` statement) anywhere in the same function in which they appear" (https://en.cppreference.com/w/c/language/goto.html), and "the entire if-statement has its own block scope" (https://en.cppreference.com/w/c/language/if.html). The encyclopedia's summary is the ordinary version: "an identifier defined in a block is not visible in outer, containing blocks, but is visible in inner, nested blocks unless it is masked by an identifier with the same name" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_(programming)). **Inference:** every one of these states *both* directions — what a block may reach and what it may not. Python even names the one case that needs a keyword to cross. This is not an exotic capability anywhere it appears; it is the definition of nesting.

**Inference — so the finding is not exoticness, it is silence, and the silence has a shape.** The Addendum's sentence "Nothing else in the language has this reach" is true **within Khayyam** and false **across languages**, and the earlier framing put the cross-language half where it did not belong. Corrected: *a block's reach over the enclosing variables is ordinary in every language examined and stated in each; in Khayyam it is ordinary in what the compiler does and stated in no document.* **Measurement — the narrower half of the silence, which the second pass did not isolate.** What the other languages state and Khayyam does not is not only "a nested block may reach outward" but the **edges**: when it needs a keyword to cross (`nonlocal`), when it may not cross at all (Python's class blocks), and whether the block may *write* what it reaches. On the last point the compiler is permissive and no document says so — `emit.ts:421` hoists every method-body `vr` as `const name$ = { value: undefined }` and the Addendum's Probe D shows a call inside a `sc` writing the enclosing cell through it. **Inference:** so the unstated thing is not the reach but the *reach's edges*, and the sharpest instance of that is a `vr` declared inside a `sc`: `docs/khayyam/variable.md:155-159` says only that a method-body variable "is not accessible outside the method", which says nothing about whether two sibling `sc`s can see each other, and the Addendum measured that they cannot. A second silence sits beside it: `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:126-131` gives the form, the inertness, and the type-level argument, and says nothing about what a scope's block may reach at all.

**Do the prohibition and the capability conflict? No — and the reason is structural, not a matter of emphasis.** `docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md:93` defines a closure *structurally*: "whatever a closure captures is, in effect, a set of unnamed fields"; "whatever a closure runs when called is, in effect, an unnamed method attached to those fields." Measured, a `sc` has **no fields of its own** — it reads the enclosing method's cells and writes through them (`emit.ts:418-422`; Addendum, Probe D) — so by that document's own test a `sc` is **not a closure**, and `:95`'s "There is no closure or lambda syntax in the grammar" is not violated in fact: no closure syntax exists, and the compiler honours that (`modules/khayyam/core/src/body.ts:205-208` reads `return` as a marker that carries nothing on its line; `emit.ts:423-429` emits a `sc` from a declaration, never from an expression). **Inference:** the two propositions are about different objects. `:95` is about a *syntax*; the capability is a *name-resolution* rule over a declaration. They cannot collide, and nothing has to be given up. What *is* in tension is the **reason** at `docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md:99` — "hidden state capturing creates invisible dependencies and breaks explicit state management" — because that reason names the property a `sc` does *not* have: every name a `sc` reaches and every name it writes is written in ordinary source in the enclosing method, in front of the reader. So `:99` is a sound reason for refusing *closure syntax* and an unsound reason to be read as a reason for refusing *reach*, and no document asks it to carry the second reading.

### Which of the two the owner should keep — settled
**Inference, and this is the research's own answer rather than the owner's to infer.** **Keep both, change neither, and add a third sentence.** Specifically:

- **Keep the capability.** It is what the compiler does (`emit.ts:418-422`, `:423-429`), it is what a branch body needs in order to reach the state its method owns, and it is the mechanism behind every language's nested-block reach.
- **Keep `docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md:95` exactly as written.** It forbids closure *syntax*, no such syntax exists, and the capability violates nothing in it. Weakening it would lose a true and load-bearing statement for no gain.
- **Do not treat `:99` as a prohibition on reach.** It is the reason for a syntax decision. If it is to be read as a rule about what bodies may capture, it must be narrowed to unnamed capture — which is what its own preceding paragraph describes. This is the only edit to `encapsulation.md` this research's evidence supports, and it is a clarification rather than a change of position.
- **Add what is owed, where the document's own test says it belongs.** The statement of the reach and of its edges goes in `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md` beside the inertness at `:129`, because under that document's own Minimal Legislation test (`:177`) the reach decides whether a program is well-formed and is therefore the grammar's to state, and `:181` names what the grammar owes a program as "the declarations a program may contain, the meanings those declarations have". The sentence owes three things the current documents say none of: that a `sc` block may read and write the enclosing method's `vr`s and parameters; that two sibling `sc`s may not see each other; and that a `return` written inside a `sc` leaves the scope (see the measurement under [How flow management is performed](#how-flow-management-is-performed-and-what-would-perform-it-without-sc)).

## Recommendation
**The concept should stay. The direction has not changed across three passes; the ground has changed twice, and this pass withdraws both earlier grounds.**

**What changed, and what is withdrawn.** The first pass made the *capability* decisive: a code scope closes over the enclosing method's variables and nothing else in the language can, so it needs a declaration form of its own. The owner has shown that half of this is false — reach is ordinary, stated by every language examined — and the third pass confirms it from five specifications rather than two. **The first ground is withdrawn.** The second pass replaced it with: *a branch must be performed, and the grammar is the only thing permitted to perform it.* The third pass reads the emitted code and shows the mechanism was never a jump but an ordinary call — so that ground is **also withdrawn**, and one of its supporting statements is withdrawn with it (a `return` inside a branch does **not** leave the enclosing method in the toolchain this repository ships; `emit.ts:431-434` emits it as `return;` inside the scope's own function).

**The ground, stated in one sentence.** **Khayyam has ruled that flow constructs are library methods (`docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:32`, `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:129`), so a conditional's branch body arrives as an argument; an argument must be a name of a declared type (`docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:131`, `docs/khayyam/method.md:71`); a name must be declared; and the only declaration a method body admits besides its own is a `sc` (`modules/khayyam/core/src/body.ts:13-23`, `:200`).**

**Inference — why this ground is better than either of the two it replaces.** Each of the earlier grounds argued that something *about branching* requires the concept. This one does not: branching requires nothing, and the evidence for that is Tcl, where a conditional is a builtin command whose bodies are its arguments and which works. What `sc` supplies is narrower and is stated by the tools themselves — `emit.ts:73-76` says a scope "is a name of the body like any other: a call names it as its own argument", and `modules/process/control-flow/if.kh:6` and `emit.test.ts:35-37` show a driver receiving exactly that. **So `sc` is the branch body's *name*, and Khayyam needs a name in that position because its conditional is not a keyword.** That also predicts the one thing the survey shows: the language that did the same thing without a block-level concept is the language that could not name the body — Tcl writes it as the command's own argument, after the condition, in the same command. A language that wants to write the driver call *first* and the body *after* it — which is what `docs/khayyam/khayyam.handoff.md:57` records the owner deciding, and what `emit.ts:46-49`'s two-pass lift exists to serve — needs the body to be a declaration. **Inference:** the concept's existence is not a claim about control flow. It is a consequence of the decision that control flow is a library, and it would not exist at all in a language that had decided control flow is syntax.

**What would reverse it.** Any one of three things, and each is a decision rather than a discovery:
1. **A first-class function value carrying a lexical attachment to the block it was written in.** Then `sc` is one spelling of that value, its category earns nothing, and the case rests on the reader's convenience alone. This is unchanged from the second pass and remains the honest reversal condition. It is foreclosed today by `docs/khayyam/encapsulation.md:95`.
2. **A ruling that flow constructs may be compiler intrinsics recognised by name.** Then a driver need not receive the body as a value at all, and nothing needs declaring. Foreclosed today by `docs/protocols/computer/compiler.md:33` and by the owner's own position.
3. **A ruling that a conditional may be a grammar keyword.** Then the body goes in a syntactic position, as in all five languages in [What `if`'s body *is*](#what-ifs-body-is-in-the-languages-the-owner-named-plus-two-more), and the named declaration is unnecessary. Foreclosed today by `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:129`.

**The file count reverses none of these**, and [what a removal would touch](#what-a-removal-would-touch--a-measurement-not-an-argument) is load-bearing for nothing.

### Concretely: what flow management looks like without `sc`, in the order an implementer meets it
1. **Today's mechanism, so the implementer knows what is being replaced.** The branch body is declared, emitted as a function inside the method, and **passed by reference to a driver as a value** (`emit.ts:423-429`, `:344`, `:356`, `:73-76`); the driver forwards it (`emit.test.ts:35-37`; emitted `Flow$OnTrue` at `control-flow.js:32-36`); and the driver is supposed to call it. **Measurement: the driver does not exist.** `Drive` is body-less (`emit.test.ts:23`; `control-flow.js:24-26`) and `link_map.json` is `{}`. Step 0 of either design is therefore the same: **write `Drive`.**
2. **Without `sc`, there is nothing to put at the argument position.** `body.ts:13-23` minus the `scope` arm leaves three commands, and `readScope` and the `tp` dispatch at `:200` go with it, so a body can no longer declare anything. The only callable the language can name is an `mt`, and a method must be declared at file level.
3. **So the branch becomes a file-level method, and every name it touches becomes a parameter.** The measured consequence: `bind` seeds a method's root block with its own parameters and nothing else (`emit.ts:52-63`), and a file-level method reaching a sibling method's local is refused. `IF(isValid, ValidData)` therefore becomes `IF(isValid, ValidBody)` where `ValidBody` is a file-level method that cannot see `validator`, `data`, or any other local of the method it was written in.
4. **The driver's signature has to restate the enclosing method's state.** Today the tree writes `tp Drive mt (self Flow) (condition Condition, branch Scope) ()` (`emit.test.ts:23`); without `sc` the branch parameter is a method, so the same method becomes `tp IF mt (self CF) (condition Condition, body Body, validator Validator, data Data) (err Error)`, and the last three names are there for no reason a reader of the conditional can see. **Inference:** this is forced by (c), which means choosing (c) settles the open question about what a signature writes in that position against the options at [What would play the role of the function type](#what-would-play-the-role-of-the-function-type-in-khayyam) — it is not a free follow-on decision.
5. **The decision mechanism is unchanged, and that is the point.** Whatever the body is, the driver's job is still to call it or not to call it, and that is ordinary code. Nothing about the mechanism requires a `sc`; only the *callee* does.
6. **Early exit does not improve.** `return` inside the branch already leaves the branch rather than the method (`emit.ts:431-434`), so every early exit is already a value the caller inspects — `Error` through an influenced group. **Inference:** the second pass listed this as a loss to `sc`-less flows; it is not one, because `sc` does not supply it either. `docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:34`'s mid-scope return and its break operation remain unimplemented either way, and a `sc` does not give a program a way to leave the enclosing method.
7. **Looping becomes a state capsule.** The step method returns the next state; a driver calls it until a terminal state. Nothing in the grammar changes — `vr`, an invocation and `return` suffice — and this is the only branch-free option that needs no new capability at all.
8. **Two protocol sentences and one grammar sentence change their referent.** `docs/protocols/process/control-flow.md:32`'s "branch-grouping primitive" has no instance in the language; `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:130` — which the emitted code already contradicts on both its jump claim and the fate of `return` — goes with the construct; `docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:145`'s "deterministic `sc` boundaries" leaves its list of five lifetime sources.
9. **What is concretely lost, in the language and not in this repository.** The branch body's lexical attachment to the method that owns the state; the ability to write the driver call above the body it names (`docs/khayyam/khayyam.handoff.md:57`, `emit.ts:46-49`); and the ability to write a branch where it is read. **Not lost:** the mechanism, which was never the construct's; and non-local exit, which the construct does not supply today.

**The dissent, restated.** This research does not say the reach is exotic — it is ordinary, and says so. It says the reach and its **edges** are unstated in the language documents while the compiler implements them, and that the sentence stating them belongs to the author of `docs/khayyam/`. This research wrote no such sentence and edited no document it names.

## Cost claims, and what survives without them
The owner held that framing a result around the cost of removal is bad research practice. This section is the audit, so a reader can check that claim rather than take it.

**Removed as arguments, in the second pass.** Option (a) was favoured because it was "the only option under which nothing in the tree moves, and the sources already compiles"; option (b) was favoured most strongly for being "the only option that (i) leaves every other thing in the tree alone". The Addendum withdrew both in place and recorded why: whether a decision is *right* cannot depend on how many files were written before it was taken.

**Removed as arguments, in this pass.** The second pass's *Recommendation* still leaned, in its "what would reverse it" and in its step 3, on two properties of the repository — that this tree would have to change, and that the sources were never written in the language's final syntax. Neither survives as an argument, and neither appears below. The second pass's step 6 listed "a `return` inside a branch can leave the enclosing method today" among the losses of a `sc`-less design; **that is withdrawn as a factual error**, not as a cost, and the loss list above is corrected accordingly.

**What survives, stated as fact and relied on for nothing.** None of these three is cited anywhere in this record's reasoning, and they are kept because the owner asked for them and because a reader weighing the decision is entitled to see what the decision distributes.
1. **A reader of a Khayyam program must understand one construct no language in this survey has as a construct, and must understand that it is inert** (`docs/khayyam/khayyam.md:129`). Property of the language as designed, paid by anyone who writes or reads Khayyam.
2. **On the `sc`-less side, a reader must hold a state machine to read what is one branch today.** Same kind of fact, and the reason [A3](#what-that-leaves-for-khayyam) is reported as changing what a reader reads rather than merely changing syntax.
3. **`docs/protocols/process/control-flow.changelog.md:124` recorded a reader-facing loss when this alternative was considered and declined** — "losing the semantic distinction between 'a data capsule' and 'a control-flow scope'". **Inference:** the survey sharpens what that sentence protects: the distinction is not a type distinction in any language examined, it is a *reader* distinction carried by the body's inertness. That is a fact about who pays, not a reason to prefer one design.

**And the one cost this pass's evidence removes entirely.** The second pass claimed the reader-facing cost of removing `sc` includes losing non-local exit from a branch. The emitted code shows `sc` supplies none (`emit.ts:431-434`), so there is no such cost to weigh in either direction.

## Open Questions
