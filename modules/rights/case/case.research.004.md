## Findings

### 1. A separate structured ruling on every claim

A duty to decide what was submitted is well supported. A duty to issue a separate structured ruling for each claim, and a ban on one disposition that rejects every claim, was not found.

Forlati states the principle *ne infra petita*: a judge or arbitrator does not decide one or several of the claims the parties raised. She treats it as part of the party-disposition principle, and as a general principle of international procedure. Her conclusion (para. 28): when claims are within jurisdiction and admissible, they in principle "deserve an answer" on the merits, and a failure to decide is a flaw in the judgment or award. She quotes Article 48(3) of the ICSID Convention: "The award shall deal with every question submitted to the Tribunal." She also quotes Article 142(2) of the ICC Rules of Procedure and Evidence: "When there is more than one charge, the Trial Chamber shall decide separately on each charge." That last sentence is the closest "decide separately" rule this pass opened. It is about charges before the International Criminal Court, as reported by Forlati, not about civil claims in one filing. This pass did not open the ICC rules themselves.

The same entry limits the duty (para. 16). Deciding some claims first (jurisdiction and admissibility) and reserving others is not a violation. A subsidiary claim is decided only if the main claim is dismissed. A decision that a claim is outside jurisdiction, or inadmissible, need not address that claim on the merits. Upholding a claim only in part does not require the award to specify the rejection of the rest: "the rest of the claim is thus deemed to be rejected, even if the judgment or award does not specify it expressly." Courts also decline some or all claims for mootness or for the sound administration of justice (paras. 23–25). So the opened scholarship requires an answer, and it also allows silence on the ungranted part of a claim, and a refusal to answer some claims.

UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules (2021), Article 34(1): the tribunal "may make separate awards on different issues at different times." That is permission to split, not a requirement of one ruling instrument per claim. Article 34(3) requires reasons unless the parties agree otherwise. Article 39(1): within 30 days, a party may request an award or an additional award "as to claims presented in the arbitral proceedings but not decided by the arbitral tribunal." Omission is a defect the same tribunal completes. It is not defined as a forbidden blanket dismissal, and the original award is not required to be a set of per-claim ruling records.

**Not found** in the sources opened: a rule that one filing's claims must each have their own structured ruling, or that a single disposition rejecting every claim is void as such.

### 2. A person × claim × role matrix

Roles are separated from the person. Binding the role to each claim was not found.

OASIS Electronic Court Filing 5.0 defines a case participant as a legal entity associated with a court case, and requires each case participant to be represented with a role element and `ecf:CaseParticipantRoleCode`. The code list constrains which codes may combine with which role element. A person may carry more than one role code (the specification history sets the person-level cardinality to zero or more). The inspected text attaches those codes to the participant in the case. **Not found** in that text: a field for the same person's role on each claim or cause of action. `CauseOfActionCode` is a separate code list on the filing, not a role axis.

Hoekstra, Breuker, Di Bello, and Boer define a role as a social role an agent plays in a context. A role prescribes behaviour in that context and is distinct from the person. The legal level of LKIF includes "typical legal roles." **Not found** in that paper: a required triple of person, claim, and role.

The organization context matches the gap and is not a source: `case` participant labels (Judge, Plaintiff, Defendant, and the rest) sit on the case–user relation, and `modules-plugable/rights/claims` holds kinds of dispute, not a role per claim.

### 3. Claim preclusion, and a larger panel instead of a bar

Clermont argues that a minimal bar is part of what a judgment is, and that how far the bar extends is policy. He does not offer a larger panel as a substitute. Omid's doubling rule was not found.

Clermont, at 1067 and 1078–80: every justice system delivers a core of res judicata (claim preclusion, glossed in the pilot as etebar-e amr-e mokhtomeh). The core is not, on his argument, a universal value. It is an institutional need. Adjudicators' judgments must bind at least to this extent: except in specified circumstances, the disappointed party cannot undo the judgment in order to change the outcome. He states the usual rationale as two ideas under "there must be an end to litigation": fairness to the winner, and cost. "No realistic conception of justice would call for pursuing truth or any other aim without concern for cost." Between a required minimum and a fairness maximum, "context-specific policy will decide how far res judicata will go." The United States, on his account, goes well beyond the minimum; other systems stay near the core. Exceptions and relief from judgment are the escapes he describes. **Not found** in this article: a proposal that re-filing the same claim against the same parties be allowed if a larger panel hears it.

Bone models frivolous suits, not re-litigation of a decided claim. He compares strict pleading, penalties, and early judicial screening, and he argues that screening on a preliminary look at the merits is the more promising of the three. Filing cost appears inside the models as a deterrent to weak suits. He reports Avery Katz for the point that, in one model of strike suits, only an indefinitely high penalty would deter every such suit. **Not found** in Bone: the use of a second, larger bench as the price of filing a claim that has already been decided.

Alarie, Green, and Iacobucci, abstract only: a formal model of optimal panel size when judicial time is scarce, with panels of five, seven, or nine, and evidence from one supreme court (1984–2005) that the authors read as consistent with the model. The abstract treats panel size as an allocation of scarce judges across appeals. It does not treat a larger panel as the condition for filing again.

Louisiana Constitution of 1974, Article V, § 8(B), is a nearby official text and not the proposal. A majority of the sitting judges must concur. In civil matters, when a district-court judgment is to be modified or reversed and one judge dissents, the case is reargued before a panel of at least five judges before judgment is rendered. That enlargement happens before rendition, inside one appeal. Five is not twice three. The text does not authorize a new filing of a decided claim.

**Not found:** a rule that re-filing the same claim against the same parties is allowed and must be heard by twice the previous number of judges, adjusted so the number is odd. Searches on doubling, on an odd panel as a filter, and on cost in place of preclusion produced the sources above and did not produce that rule.

### 4. How cost is computed, and how judges are assigned

Opened standards do compute cost, and they do describe assignment. They do not use the words "kernel" and "pluggable." They do put fee schedules, fee queries, and assignment criteria in a layer that can vary without rewriting the claim.

Two different "costs" appear, and they should not be collapsed.

Party-facing cost. UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules (2021), Article 40: the tribunal fixes the costs of arbitration in the final award, and may do so in another decision. Costs are a closed list: each arbitrator's fees, arbitrators' expenses, tribunal-required expert and assistance costs, approved witness expenses, parties' legal and other costs to the extent the tribunal finds them reasonable, and appointing-authority and PCA fees. Article 41(1): arbitrators' fees and expenses "shall be reasonable in amount, taking into account the amount in dispute, the complexity of the subject matter, the time spent by the arbitrators and any other relevant circumstances of the case." Article 41(2): if an appointing authority applies a schedule or method, the tribunal takes it into account "to the extent that it considers appropriate." Article 41(4)(a): when fees are fixed, the tribunal explains how the amounts were calculated. The formula is inside the procedural rules, and the schedule is an external input the rules point to.

OASIS Electronic Court Filing 5.0, § 6.1.3: if court policy enables it, the filer's system may call `GetFeesCalculation` with the filing message and receive the fee and an optional list of charges. The step "MAY be omitted if there are no fees associated with filings in the court or the calculated fees are already known." § 6.1.4: processing of `ReviewFiling` "is dependent on court policy." Fee calculation is specified as a court-policy operation beside the filing, not as part of the definition of a claim.

Institutional cost. CEPEJ(2016)12, paragraph 69, is a performance indicator, not a fee. Method one: divide the total cost of a unit by the number of cases resolved, giving an average cost per procedure. Method two: attach to each case the hours of the judge and staff and the expenses incurred. **Not found** in that paragraph: a rule for the fee a party must pay, or a rule that this indicator is outside procedure.

Assignment. The CEPEJ case-weighting study (2019–2020) distinguishes the weight of a case type, used to estimate how many judges a caseload needs, from the evaluation of an individual judge. Among systems that did not report a weighting system, the study records Poland's report of a "random case allocation system" (SLPS) since 2018, which assigns cases to judges under pre-established criteria for a balanced distribution of case types. That is one reported national system inside a comparative study. **Not found** in the study: a general assignment algorithm, or a claim that assignment is either essential procedure or optional policy.

**Not found:** the vocabulary "kernel module" versus "pluggable module" applied to cost or to assignment. The nearest structural split this pass opened is ECF's court-policy switch for fees and review, and UNCITRAL's pointer to an external fee schedule.

### 5. An earlier decision that stays and is outvoted

Opened models aggregate the votes of one panel, sitting together, by majority or by a chosen protocol. They do not keep a rendered decision in a later pool so that new judges outvote it.

UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules (2021), Article 33(1): when there is more than one arbitrator, "any award or other decision of the arbitral tribunal shall be made by a majority of the arbitrators." Article 33(2) lets the presiding arbitrator decide a procedural question alone only when there is no majority or the tribunal authorizes it, subject to revision by the tribunal. Article 34(2): awards are final and binding. The majority is of the arbitrators then constituted. **Not found** in these articles: an average of their decisions, or a later enlargement that leaves the earlier award standing as a minority vote.

Kornhauser and Sager (1993) address a fixed collegial court and two ways of summing votes already cast: sum the votes on the outcome of the case, or sum the votes on each issue and then combine those results. The two protocols can disagree. They argue that neither protocol is always superior, and that the court should choose the protocol by a "metavote." The votes in their model are contemporaneous votes of one panel. **Not found** in the opened portion of the article: a rule that a judgment already issued stays valid, counts in a later average, and becomes a minority when judges are added. A footnote points to Kornhauser, "Modeling Collegial Courts I: Path Dependence", 12 International Review of Law and Economics 169 (1992). That article was not opened, so its content is not a finding here.

List (August 2009 manuscript) restates the same paradox as the start of judgment-aggregation theory, and treats proposition-wise majority voting as the rule that can produce an inconsistent collective judgment. The aggregation he describes is of individual judgments held at one time. **Not found** in the opened pages: a growing panel, an arithmetic mean of judicial votes, or a judgment that is never reversed because it is merely outvoted.

Fourth Circuit Appellate Procedure Guide, "Panel Judgment Vacated": granting rehearing or rehearing en banc "vacates the previous panel judgment and opinion"; the rehearing reviews the judgment from which review was sought, "not a review of the judgment of the panel." That is one court's practice, and it is the opposite of a retained vote: the earlier judgment is vacated rather than kept as a minority.

**Not found:** a model in which an earlier judge's decision remains valid, enters an average or a later majority, and is not overturned but becomes a minority.

### 6. A registry inquiry as a claim that unlocks later claims

**Not found:** a pre-filing inquiry to a registry modeled as itself a claim, requiring a judge's acceptance before later claims leave draft status.

Nearby, and different:

- Forlati, paragraph 16: a court may decide jurisdiction and admissibility first and reserve the merits. That preliminary decision is a stage of the same proceeding. It is not described as a registry inquiry, and the later claims are not described as drafts awaiting that ruling.
- OASIS Electronic Court Filing 5.0: `ReviewFiling` is the court's receipt of a filing. If the clerk reviews and accepts it, the filing is sent on to be docketed. Rejection is a clerk or court-record outcome on the filing, not a judicial ruling on a registry-inquiry claim. `GetCase` and `GetCaseList` are queries available once the case is accessible. They are not claims.

The organization claims module, read as context only, does not contain this inquiry-claim.

## Questions for Omid

1. **Ruling edge on a claim.** Opened sources require an answer to each admissible claim, allow that answer to sit in one instrument, and allow the ungranted part of a claim to count as rejected without being stated. Must each claim in one filing carry its own ruling edge, and is one disposition that rejects every claim a valid ruling on each of them? This blocks whether a missing per-claim ruling is a validation error.

2. **Person × claim × role.** Opened standards put a role on a participant in the case, not on each claim. Is a person × claim × role triple required on the petition, and is the judge the only role, with every other person a user on the claim? This blocks the `participant` edge on `case`.

3. **Refiling and panel size.** The doubling rule was not found. State the number of judges for the first, second, and third filing of the same claim by the same parties, given that twice an odd number can be even, and state whether any later filing is refused. This blocks the refiling constraint.

4. **Cost and assignment.** State the inputs that determine the cost charged for a filing, state who selects the judges, and state whether each of those two rules is a kernel module or a pluggable module. This blocks placement under `modules/` or `modules-plugable/`.

5. **Aggregation of an earlier decision.** When judges are added, does the earlier judge's decision remain a vote in a majority count, or a quantity in an average, and does the earlier ruling edge stay in the graph as a minority? This blocks the aggregation rule on the ruling edge.

6. **Registry inquiry as a claim.** Which registry inquiry is the first claim, what proposition the judge must accept, and which later claims stay draft until that acceptance exists? This blocks the draft-status constraint and the identity of that claim.
