# The Dead Among the Living

## Abstract
How the dead's remains and their memorials should be situated on a society's land is, in this ledger, answered once and in one voice: an ecological burial ([Ecological burial (Green Burial) and the necropolis of notables with a planted tree in place of a headstone](the-dead-among-the-living.md#ecological-burial-green-burial-and-the-necropolis-of-notables-with-a-planted-tree-in-place-of-a-headstone)) in which the body, without coffin or embalming chemicals, passes through natural humus composting, one sturdy tree is planted over each grave, the headstone and every impervious surface fall to 0 percent, and the cemetery becomes — by arithmetic, not metaphor — the ground-plane forest that the living keep walking. The answer's own Fit text is the page's sharpest finding: imposed on the existing fabric, this doctrine "runs into the deadlock of traditional religious law, the resistance of burial institutions and lack of ground," and so the requirement's question, which speaks of the dead a society already has and the memorials its families already visit, is answered here only for the settlement that is still to come — and only if a second, marker-side answer (the QR plaque replacing the stone, which needs no new grave to be true) is read into the mechanism rather than found written beside it.

## Introduction

### Motivation
This page is a requirement kept whole on purpose: the burial question was recorded once, and splitting its consequences across other ledgers — the forest of the ground plane, the protection of the soil bed, the open-ground stability of the 20/80 rule — would hide the one thing the record makes visible, that the society's answer about its dead is load-bearing for its answer about its living land, and that the ledger currently holds a single such answer. A reader weighing the cemetery question against the existing settlement's dead (whose graves are already stone, whose rites are already law) finds no rival record here to argue with; that absence is itself the record of this batch's most consequential gap, and it is stated as a question the owner must rule on — whether the requirement is settled for new land only, or whether the same mechanism, applied at the headstone instead of the grave, already contains its retrofit half.

## Explanation

### The problem the answers share
How should the remains of the dead and their memorials be situated on the society's land? The question carries three obligations that pull against each other: the dead must remain among the living in some durable, visitable form (a memorial, a name, a place); the ground they occupy must keep living (soil, water, roots, not a sealed slab); and whatever the society's rites require of a body must actually be done. One record answers with a single object — a tree over a composted grave, its identity carried by a small biometric plaque — which collapses the memorial into information and the cemetery into forest; the obligations' real weights (ritual law's claim on the body's treatment, the family's claim on the stone's place) are the places where any reader will press, and the record's own Fit critique says, honestly, that pressed in the existing fabric it is "rejected as a cultural conflict."

### Approaches

#### Ecological burial (Green Burial) and the necropolis of notables with a planted tree in place of a headstone
**Mechanism:** Remove the traditional concrete cemeteries with their heat-absorbing black stones. The body of the deceased, without coffins or embalming chemicals, decomposes through a natural humus composting process, and over each grave the sapling of one sturdy tree is planted — a symbol of the continuation of the cycle of life — so the graveyard turns into a living, verdant forest. The doctrine covers animals as well as people — it is the burial of «انسان ها و حیوانات خانگی» (humans and pets), the business plan names, in the very same non-stable ways that leave biological pollution at the grave and demand timber, shroud and stone — and it is explicitly the model of the new international startups it cites by name (Transcend, Tree Burial Pods, Recompose), in two variants: the body placed, in a fully recyclable package with the tree's own nutrient matter and sapling, into a conventional-shaped grave; or the soil-underneath process accelerated — the months that human composting takes under the ground reduced by scientific means to under a few days — so the microbial and viral pollution of the body falls to near zero and the compost left behind is ideal for the tree's growth.
**Metrics:** Headstone surface and impervious cover: 0 percent; trees added per deceased: 1 sturdy tree; emissions of concrete and stone pollutants: zero; microbial and viral load of the composted remains: near zero; composting time: from months of natural decay to under a few days where the accelerated method is chosen; scope: human and household-animal burials alike.
**Prerequisites:** soil with active biological texture; small anti-corrosion biometric QR plaques on the tree trunks in place of heavy carved stones.
**Conventional-model flaw:** Hectares of fertile ground held by thick black-granite stones and concrete, the plain's soil made sterile, and a visual depression induced on the settlement.

### How the approaches collide and compose
**The record's mechanism is two answers wearing one coat — the grave and the marker — and only one of them the Fit critique condemns.** The composting burial ("without coffins or embalming chemicals... humus composting") is the half that meets the Fit text's named obstacles: it re-regulates what a religiously governed body may be done to, and it needs the ground the existing fabric does not have. But the same record's prerequisites carry a different instrument — small anti-corrosion biometric QR plaques "in place of heavy carved stones" — which operates on the memorial, not the body: a settlement that keeps its conventional burial rite could still retire black granite, lift the 0-percent impervious metric on existing cemetery land, and plant a sturdy tree per grave as the headstone's replacement without disturbing a single deceased. This is one question with two answers for the owner: does [Ecological burial (Green Burial) and the necropolis of notables with a planted tree in place of a headstone](the-dead-among-the-living.md#ecological-burial-green-burial-and-the-necropolis-of-notables-with-a-planted-tree-in-place-of-a-headstone) stand or fall as one indivisible rite (so that its greenfield verdict, read literally, condemns also the plaque and the tree to the new land, and the existing fabric keeps its heat-absorbing stones until reverse renewal empties it)? Or is it severable into a soil answer and a memorial answer, the second of which the Fit text never actually indicts — in which case the "0 percent impervious headstone surface" is a guarantee the existing settlement could be buying with gardeners and plaques, starting now? The evidence the two answers argue from is in the record's own words: the Fit text names "the deadlock of traditional religious law, the resistance of burial institutions and lack of ground" — all three bear on the body and the land, none on the stone.

**Where this single answer sits among the ledger's other land claims, it composes without surplus — and without defense.** The cemetery-for-doctrine is the ground-plane forest's quietest supplier: every deceased adds one sturdy tree and removes one impervious square, so the record is the only place in the society's land arithmetic where the population's end increases the tree canopy rather than its footprint, and its synergy lines say exactly that (continuous forest; protected soil; the 20/80 rule's open ground). But the composition has no metric behind it: "1 tree per deceased" is a flow, not a stock, and the page records no cemetery area, no rotation of ground, and no rule for what happens to a memorial tree that dies — nor, since the necropolis of notables shares the mechanism, any answer to who decides which dead are buried under public canopy and which under family trees. The sharpest unresolved question the set leaves: the record answers for both halves of the requirement's phrase — "remains" and "memorials" — yet it is written for neither the old settlement's dead nor the rites of the living who visit them, so any adoption of it is an adoption of a timeline, not a policy, until the severability ruling above says otherwise.
