# Transport at the Boundary of the Network

## Abstract
The boundary has one recorded answer in this ledger, and it is a double commitment. Conventional cars may not enter the society's domain: the resident's or guest's vehicle drives only to the settlement's entry, where the outer conventional roads meet the network, and there is surrendered to tiered parking built on the "long-term dormancy" pattern — three cars deep per single one-way lane, chosen for maximum storage density — after which people continue on shared fleets and the aerial network. The same record simultaneously sizes itself to sleep the cars of 50 thousand residents plus moderate tourism, and promises that if car dependence later falls the structures "immediately" change use to controlled indoor farms. It also names its own failure mode: a hub without a direct connection to a fast public fleet ends with cars abandoned in the surrounding residential lanes. The requirement this record answers — where our network meets the outer conventional systems — is therefore settled here in one form and held open in another: whether the gate tower is a permanent institution of car storage or insured capital against a dependence the network is designed to dissolve, the record bets both ways in one page.

## Introduction

### Motivation
The boundary is where two networks touch, so everything inland quietly leans on what this one page asserts: the goods ledger's depot lists the entry parking as its prerequisite, the human-travel network counts these hubs among its synergies, and the zero-percent conventional-street metric in the network record presupposes that whatever cars remain are stopped exactly here. Filed nowhere else, the hub's load-bearing assumptions — a resident fleet that exists, stacking dense enough to matter, a conversion clause that is real rather than rhetorical — would be consumed silently by three other requirements while their own verdicts assumed the opposite. Keeping them stated once, here, with their dependencies named, is what lets the audit check a shared premise instead of discovering three private ones.

## Explanation

### The problem the answers share
How shall our automated network connect with the outer, conventional transport systems — what does an entering car do, and where does a passenger board? The axes are set by the question itself: where the car stops (the gate, or nowhere), what carries people from the stop inward (a shared fleet, the aerial network, both), and what the gate structure becomes if the question it answers eventually disappears. The need — a workable seam between the society's fabric and the conventional world outside it — is not any of the answers and survives all of them.

### Approaches

#### Centralized long-dormancy parking hubs at the entry gates with compact 3-car-deep stacking
**Mechanism:** Entry of any conventional vehicle into the society's domain is prohibited. Residents' or guests' private cars are permitted to move only to the settlement's entry (at the junction with the unpaved, conventional intercity roads) and are quartered in dedicated multi-tier parking structures. These parking houses are designed on the pattern of long-term vehicle dormancy (Long-term Stacking): three cars parked one behind another per single one-way access lane, so that maximum storage density is realized. If dependence on the car falls in the future, these structures immediately convert to controlled-covered agricultural farms (Indoor Farming). The prohibition is reached through a transition, not a switch: while private cars are still being used, the demand lever is a per-day toll on personal-car travel — «در صورت تمایل به استفاده از اتومبیل شخصی به ازای هر روز تردد عوارض خاصی اخذ شود» — a special levy exacted for each day of use, which prices the dependence during the years the hub still has to exist.
**Metrics:** Capacity matched to 50 thousand resident population and moderate tourism; 3 cars in depth per one-way access lane; 100 percent flexibility for conversion to covered agricultural farm; transitional demand lever: a per-day personal-car-use toll while conventional cars are still in service.
**Prerequisites:** A fleet of shared rental cars for residents to leave the settlement; a smart registration and tracking system for vehicle exits according to demand timing.
**Conventional-model flaw:** Municipal rules that impose 1 to 2 mandatory parking places per dwelling unit in each apartment's basement — encouraging car purchase, local traffic, and the heating of the ground beneath.

### How the approaches collide and compose
With one answer on file, the collision this record surfaces is inside its own text, and it is a single owner decision with two answers: is the long-dormancy hub an institution of car storage or insurance against the car? The institution reading takes the record's arithmetic at face value — capacity "matched to 50 thousand residents and moderate tourism" is a demand commitment, the 3-car-deep single-lane stacking is its efficient expression, and the conversion clause is disposal advice should the bet fail; on this reading the structure is engineered parking-first (ceilings, ramps, ventilation for exhaust), and the farm is a future, expensive retrofit. The insurance reading takes the record's own enemy list at face value — it indicts municipal parking for "encouraging car purchase" and heating the ground — and treats the hub as a transitional instrument whose normal end-state is the farm; then designing parking-first is the error, and the clear heights, floor loads and water that a controlled-covered farm needs must be bought now, at some cost in parking density, so that "immediately converts" is a switch and not a second demolition. The two answers diverge on everything downstream: a 3-deep, one-way, long-stay slot is first-in-last-out retrieval by design, so the institution version must staff or robotize exit sequencing (its tracking-system prerequisite hints at scheduling, never at who or what moves the inner cars), while the insurance version's conversion cost is a capital line in this requirement's ledger, not a future project. The evidence each side would weigh: measured retrieval delay under the demand timing the prerequisite assumes, and the cost delta between a parking section and a farm-capable dual section at the gate.

As composition, the record is load-bearing for neighbors and honest about it: the goods depot lists it as a prerequisite for its peripheral parking, the aerial network counts it as the place cars sleep instead of crossing the fabric, and the shared-restaurant line names the same asset-sharing logic — cars dorming at the gate the way kitchens leave the suites. What the composition requires the ledger to notice: the hub exists only if some conventional traffic keeps arriving — its fleet and tracking prerequisites bind whoever owns the outer roads — and if the network ledger's own ambition (zero percent conventional streets inside) succeeds completely, the 100-percent-flexibility clause is not a promise but the plan.
